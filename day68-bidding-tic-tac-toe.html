GAMEFROMZERO · DAY 68

🪙 Bidding Tic-Tac-Toe — the first game here with no side to move. Nobody is "on turn": before every mark both players seal a bid, the higher bid wins the right to place, and the winner pays that bid to the loser, so the money never leaves the table — it only changes sides. Take the turn variable out of the state and minimax has nothing to alternate, so the engine stops being a search and becomes a price. The value of a position is the share of the pot you need before you can force your line, and it satisfies one line of arithmetic: it is the average of your best move's price and their best move's price, and half the gap between those two is exactly what you should bid. Solve that on the board's DAG and every number falls out as a whole coin from a 512-coin pot, because a game is at most nine auctions and nine halvings is 512. And the same integers answer a completely different question: flip a fair coin to decide who marks next, and the price of a position is the probability you lose it. Then the part that is measured rather than claimed — a second solver that plays the auction for real with 512 whole chips and enumerates every integer bid, sharing no arithmetic with the tablebase, reproduces all 18,753 prices exactly. And ordinary tic-tac-toe's famous draw survives here in a band 20 coins wide out of 512: outside it, the game is decided by the bankroll before a mark is placed. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, offline.

Delete the turn, and a game becomes a market


      
Sixty-seven days of engines have all had the same first line of state: whose turn is
      it. Minimax alternates on it, alpha-beta prunes against it, the tablebase indexes by
      it. Bidding tic-tac-toe deletes it. There is a pot of coins split between the two players,
      and before every single mark both of them write down a bid. The higher bid wins the right
      to place — and pays that bid to the other player. Nothing is ever spent.
      The pot is constant; only the split moves.


      
So a position is just a board. Not a board plus a mover. And the question you can ask
      about it is not "who is winning" but how much money would I need to be holding, right
      now, to guarantee my line. That number is a price.


      
The whole engine is one identity


      
R(b) = ( min over MY placements R  +  max over THEIR placements R ) / 2
bid   = ( max over THEIR placements R  -  min over MY placements R ) / 2


      
Read it as a market rather than as a search. The two children that matter are the best
      thing that could happen next and the worst thing that could happen next. The price of the
      position sits exactly halfway between them, because the auction is a fair coin you are
      allowed to pay to bias. And what you should bid is exactly what winning is worth: half the
      distance between those two futures.


      
The proof that this is not just a plausible formula is one line of bookkeeping. Suppose
      you hold more than R(b) and you bid b = (max−min)/2.


      
    
        
  • You win the auction: you pay b, and you move to the child whose price is
        R − b. Your surplus is unchanged.
    • 
        
  • They win the auction with something bigger than b: they hand you more than
        b, and the worst board they can hand you costs R + b. Your
        surplus is unchanged, or better.
    • 
      


      
Either branch preserves "I am above the price", and the board fills in nine marks, so it
      ends. That is the entire strategy, and note what it does not contain: any reference
      to what the opponent bid. You do not react to the auction. You quote a price.


      
Why the pot is 512 and not 100


      
Every value here is a dyadic rational. A board with k marks has at most
      9 − k halvings left in it, so its price is a whole multiple of
      2k out of 2⁹. Make the pot 512 coins and every price and every optimal
      bid is a whole number of coins — no floats, no epsilon comparisons, no
      rounding anywhere in the tablebase. Make the pot 100 and you are immediately doing
      floating-point arithmetic on a game that has an exact integer answer. The page asserts the
      multiple-of-2k property on all 18,753 reachable boards; it is not a
      decoration, it is why /2 in the recurrence never rounds.


      
The state space is much bigger than tic-tac-toe's


      
Ordinary tic-tac-toe has 5,478 reachable positions, and the reason it is that small is
      that the counts of X and O never differ by more than one. That rule does not exist here.
      Whoever pays can move, and a rich player can win eight auctions in a row, so
      eight – nil is a legal board. Reachable positions:
      18,753. Prune with the alternation assumption and two-thirds of the game
      quietly stops existing, and every price you compute for the third that remains is wrong,
      because its children were.


      
Two tablebases, and the draw band between them


      
A price is defined against a single objective, so a draw has to count as a loss for
      whoever is being priced. That means one tablebase cannot describe this game. You need two:
      the price of an X line, and the price of an O line, solved separately.
      From the empty board both come out at 266 of 512 — 133/256, about 51.95%.


      

        
Two prices summing to more than the pot is exactly a
        draw band: bankrolls where neither player can buy their line.
        266 + 266 − 512 = 20 coins. Hold 247 to 266 and tic-tac-toe is
        still the drawn game everybody knows. Hold 267 and you can force a line; hold 246 and the
        bot can. The classic result "tic-tac-toe is a draw" turns out to be true on 20 of the 513
        possible bankrolls — 3.9% — and false on the other 96.1%.

      


      
The page does not assert that; it plays it. All 513 stakes, two perfect players,
      513 real games, against the two tablebases' prediction: 513 agreements, 0 disagreements.
      When one side is far enough above its price the game ends in three marks,
      which happens on 247 of the 513 stakes.


      
The same integers, as a coin flip


      
Take all the money away. Instead, before each mark, flip a fair coin to decide who
      places, and let both sides play optimally. The value of a position under that rule obeys


      
W(b) = ½·(best I can do)  +  ½·(worst they can do to me)


      
which is the same recurrence with the same boundary values, so it produces the same
      512-based integers. That is the Peres–Schramm–Sheffield–Wilson
      random-turn identity, and it says something worth stopping on: the price of a
      position is the probability you lose it when turn order is decided by coin flips.
      266/512 is not only "the bankroll you need"; it is "you lose 51.95% of coin-flip games from
      the empty board".


      
The page is deliberate about what that check is worth. Written as two programs it is
      still one recurrence, so agreement between them is an identity and not evidence. What
      is evidence is flipping the coins: 200,000 random-turn games with both sides
      playing the random-turn table, and the measured failure rate walking to 0.5195.


      
The check that is not a restatement


      
So the real verification is a solver that plays the auction literally. Put
      512 whole chips on the table. A state is (board, my chips). Enumerate every
      integer bid I could make and every integer bid they could make, resolve the auction, pay,
      recurse. There is no averaging in it, no division by two, and no notion of a "price" — just
      quantifiers over bids.


      
I commit my bid first, they answer   :   ∃ b  ∀ g   (pessimistic)
they commit first, I answer          :   ∀ g  ∃ b   (optimistic)


      
Two versions, because sealed simultaneous bids would otherwise need a mixed strategy
      before the value is even defined, and the true sealed-bid value is trapped between them.
      Measured over 9.6 million (board, chip-split) states: the two are identical.
      This auction never needs to randomise.


      

        
And the chip solver's threshold is the tablebase price, exactly, on
        every one of the 18,753 boards, in all four combinations of "who is being priced" and
        "who wins ties". Where ties go against you the threshold is price + 1 — you must
        beat the price. Where ties go to you it is the price itself — you only have to
        match it. Two solvers with no arithmetic in common, 75,012 board comparisons,
        0 disagreements.

      


      
The bug that only the exhaustive sweep could find


      

        
I wrote the perfect player's decision as "if my purse beats my price,
        buy the line; otherwise defend against theirs" — the same strict comparison for both
        sides. It wins 512 of the 513 stakes. The one it loses is the exact boundary, where the
        bot holds precisely its own price, and the reason is the tie-break: the bot wins tied
        auctions, so it only needs to match its price, while I have to beat mine. With
        both tests strict the bot spends that game defending a position it could have been
        buying, and hands back a forced win as a draw. One coin, one game in 513, and no
        amount of self-play would have surfaced it — only playing every stake.

The measurement: which half of the strategy matters


      
A Richman strategy is two things — the bid, and the square. Nothing in the theory says
      which is carrying the game, so the page runs it as an A/B against one fixed opponent (a bot
      that shoves its whole purse whenever a line is one mark away and plays the usual
      block-then-centre heuristic), 600 games each from random bankrolls, sides swapped.


      

        
Keep the correct bid and pick a uniformly random square:
        80.2%. Keep the correct square and make a uniformly random
        bid: 56.0%. In this game the auction is worth far more than the
        board. Win enough consecutive auctions and where you put the marks stops mattering much;
        lose the auctions and knowing the perfect square is close to worthless.

      


      
Two smaller results from the same ladder. Bidding a flat sixteen coins every time —
      exactly the sort of "sensible constant" that looks harmless — wins 2%, far
      worse than bidding at random, because a constant bidder is trivially outbid at the only
      moments that matter. And bidding half your purse, which sounds reckless, wins 66.7%: it is a
      crude approximation of the real rule, which is that the bid should scale with what is at
      stake, and a purse-proportional bid is at least correlated with that.


      
The control that makes those numbers mean anything


      
A perfect player starting above its own price must never fail to make its line, whatever
      it is played by. 900 games against all fifteen combinations of the five bidders and three
      movers on this page: 0 failures. If that control ever broke, the story would be the harness
      and not the policy.


    

  






  

    


      
🔨 How it is built


      
1. A board is a base-3 integer, and that is the whole index

      
const code = sum over cells of value * 3^i;      // 0 empty, 1 X, 2 O
place(code, cell, who) === code + who * POW[cell];

      
Nine cells, 19,683 codes, and a move is one addition. Every table on this page is a flat
      typed array indexed by that integer — no maps, no string keys, no board copying. The 930
      codes that are not reachable cost 930 bytes and buy an index with no arithmetic in it.


      
2. The topological order is a bucket sort on mark count

      
for (let c = 0; c < n; c++) bucket[marks[c]].push(c);
for (let k = cells; k >= 0; k--) ...                 // 9 marks first, empty board last

      
Every move adds a mark, so descending mark count is a topological order of the
      game DAG. One linear pass solves the whole tablebase — no recursion, no memo dictionary, no
      stack depth to worry about. The same order solves the random-turn table.


      
3. Reachability, and the assumption that would have wrecked it

      
// either colour may be placed from any live board - there is no alternation here
for (const cell of empty) for (let who = 1; who <= 2; who++) push(place(code, cell, who));

      
The obvious tic-tac-toe optimisation is that X's count minus O's count is 0 or 1. In a
      bidding game that is false, because winning an auction is what earns a move and money can
      buy several in a row. Reachable boards: 18,753, not 5,478.


      
4. The solver, which is six lines of real work

      
let mn = Infinity, mx = -Infinity;
for (const i of emptyCells(c)){
  mn = Math.min(mn, R[place(c, i, goal)]);      // I place: I want the price LOW
  mx = Math.max(mx, R[place(c, i, foil)]);      // they place: they want it HIGH
}
R[c]   = (mn + mx) / 2;
bid[c] = (mx - mn) / 2;

      
Both children are multiples of 2marks+1, so both divisions are exact and the
      whole table stays in Int32Array. The page re-checks that identity on the
      finished table rather than trusting the loop that produced it: 22,186 checks, 0 violations,
      including that RX + RO is never below the pot — two players
      cannot both be able to buy their own line out of the same board.


      
5. Offence, defence, and one comparison that must not be strict

      
function offence(s, mine, own){
  return s.me === 2 ? (mine >= own.R[s.code]) : (mine > own.R[s.code]);
}

      
O wins tied auctions, so O only needs to match its price and X has to beat its own. Write
      both as > and the perfect player draws a game it had won, at exactly one
      stake in 513. The chip oracle independently confirms which side of each boundary is
      strict — that is not a style choice, it is a measurable fact about the tie rule.


      
6. The oracle: quantifiers, then the same quantifiers fast

      
// literal: every bid against every bid, O(chips^2) per state
for (let b = 0; b <= mine; b++)
  for (let g = 0; g <= theirs; g++)
    ... (tieToFoil ? b > g : b >= g) ? AX[t-b] : AO[t+g] ...

// fast: a prefix-OR and a suffix-AND are monotone, so each has ONE crossover index
pess = tieToFoil ? (t >= s0) || (t >= p0+1 && 2*t >= s0+p0)
                 : (t >= p0) && (2*t+1 >= s0+p0);

      
The fast form is a rewrite of the quantifiers, not of the game — it still knows nothing
      about halving. It is checked against the literal double loop on every state of the
      two toy boards, both objectives, both tie rules, before being used at 512 chips where the
      literal version would be 1.6 billion inner iterations.


      
7. Toy boards that exist only to be oracles

      
specRow5 : 5 cells in a line, 3-in-a-row wins, pot 32, 243 codes
specTiny2: 2x2, any 2 cells are a line, pot 16,  81 codes

      
Small enough that the literal solver runs the full cross-product of bids in milliseconds,
      and structurally different enough (one has overlapping lines, the other has nothing but
      lines) that a bug in line detection or in the DAG order shows up in one of them.


      
8. The invariant nobody thinks to check

      
if (xWins) x -= bx; else x += bo;      // the LOSER is paid, always

      
Get the sign wrong here and you have written a game where money is destroyed, the pot
      shrinks, and every price on the page is quietly meaningless while the engine still looks
      fine. 400 random games between random policies, 2,158 auctions, 0 bankrolls outside
      [0, 512].

10. What this engine is not

      
// Exact over the whole state space, so the limits ARE the state space: 3^cells
// boards, and the chip oracle is boards x chips. 3x3 against a 512-chip pot is
// 19,683 x 513 bytes twice over and about a second; 4x4 is 43M boards and is not
// a web page. Nothing here approximates, and nothing here searches - the price of
// a position is read off a table, never looked for.

      
Stated in the code next to the limit rather than in the copy, because it is a property of
      the implementation and not a disclaimer.
