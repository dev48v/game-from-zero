Nine Men's Morris — three phases, one board, and a capture rule with an exception that decides whole games.

The whole game, against a real search

Place nine pieces each, one at a time. Line three of your own up along one of the board's sixteen printed lines and you have a mill, which lets you take an enemy piece off the board. When the hands are empty you slide along the lines instead, and a player down to exactly three pieces may fly anywhere. You lose at two pieces, or the moment you have no legal move at all.

What the board actually is

Nothing on this page stores a picture. The board is sixteen lines of three point numbers, and every other fact — which points touch, how many lines run through a point, what a legal slide is — is derived from that list and checked below.

Step 1 — the board is a graph, not a grid

Every grid game you have written so far indexed cells by row and column, and neighbours came from adding one to a coordinate. Nine Men's Morris breaks that immediately: its twenty-four points sit on three nested squares, and the corner of the outer square has two neighbours while the middle of the middle square has four. If you store it as a 7×7 array you will spend the rest of the build writing special cases for the twenty-five holes. Store it instead as twenty-four numbered points plus an adjacency list, and every rule becomes a list lookup. The drawing is then a separate concern that never touches the rules.

Step 2 — the sixteen lines come first, and adjacency falls out of them

There is a temptation to type the adjacency list by hand, and that is how boards get silently wrong: one missing pair and a legal move disappears for the rest of the project. The lines are the shorter and more memorable object — four along each of the three squares, plus the four spokes that join the squares at their midpoints, sixteen in all. Each line of three points contributes exactly two adjacent pairs, the first-to-second and the second-to-third. That gives thirty-two edges with no hand-typing, and it guarantees the two structures can never disagree, because one of them is computed from the other.

Step 3 — every point lies on exactly two lines

This one is worth checking rather than assuming, because it is the reason mill detection is cheap. When a piece lands on a point you do not scan all sixteen lines to see whether a mill appeared; you look at the two lines that pass through that point and nothing else. Twenty-four points on two lines each is forty-eight memberships, and sixteen lines of three points is also forty-eight, so the count closes exactly. An index from point to its lines is built once at start-up and every mill test afterwards is two array reads. The same index is what makes the capture rule affordable, since that rule asks the mill question about every enemy piece on the board.

Step 4 — three phases, and they are not three games

Players often describe Nine Men's Morris as placing, then moving, then flying, as if the program needed three move generators. It needs one, with a question at the top: if you still have pieces in hand you place on any empty point, otherwise you move, and a mover with exactly three pieces left ignores adjacency. Phase is therefore not stored anywhere — it is a function of the pieces in hand and the pieces on the board, so it can never drift out of step with the position. The numbers below come from real states the engine built, not from prose. Notice how the branching factor collapses once the hands empty, and then explodes again the moment someone starts to fly.

Step 5 — a mill does not end your turn, it extends it

This is the single detail that breaks a first implementation. Forming a mill does not hand the turn over; it obliges you to remove an enemy piece, and only then does the opponent move. If you model a turn as "one move, then swap sides", there is nowhere for the removal to live, and people usually bolt it on as a modal prompt outside the engine — at which point the search cannot see captures at all. The fix is to make the removal a move like any other, and to let the state carry a pending flag that says the same player must move again. Move generation then returns removals instead of placements while that flag is set, and the search needs no special case whatsoever. 


    

  


  

    
Step 6 — the capture exception, which is the rule people get wrong

    

      You may not take an enemy piece that is standing inside a mill — unless every
      single enemy piece is inside a mill, in which case the protection lapses and you may
      take any of them. Drop the exception and a player who gets all their pieces into
      mills becomes literally invulnerable and the game never ends. Implement it as a
      filter with a fallback and it is three lines: collect the enemy pieces that are not
      in a mill, and if that list is empty, return all of them instead. The table below
      runs both halves on real positions, including the inverted case where the filter
      returns nothing and the fallback has to fire.
    

    

    

    

  


  

    
Step 7 — flying is a rule about count, not about a phase flag

    

      A player reduced to exactly three pieces may move any one of them to any empty
      point. It is a mercy rule: with three pieces and adjacency you would usually be
      frozen, and the game would end by blocking rather than by capture. For the engine it
      matters because it changes the branching factor more than anything else in the game.
      Three sliding pieces might have six moves between them; three flying pieces have
      three times however many points are empty, which late in a game is still a large
      number. The table measures both on the same positions so the jump is a number rather
      than a claim.
    

    

    

  


  

    
Step 8 — there are two ways to lose, and the second one is real

    

      The obvious loss is being ground down to two pieces, since two pieces can never make
      a mill. The other is being blocked: if it is your turn and you have no legal move,
      you lose on the spot. Beginners treat blocking as a curiosity, but against a search
      it is a standard winning plan, because a wall of enemy pieces is easier to build
      than a sequence of captures. In code the two conditions want to live in one place,
      checked after every single move including the removal half of a mill turn. The
      engine does exactly that, and the table shows both endings reached from constructed
      positions.
    

    

  


  

    
Step 9 — without draw rules the game does not terminate

    

      Two players with no way through each other will shuffle the same two pieces back and
      forth forever, and so will two copies of a deterministic search. Real play uses two
      stoppers: the same position occurring three times is a draw, and fifty moves by each
      side with no capture is a draw. Both need the engine to carry history, which is the
      first time a position alone stops being enough state. The repetition key has to
      include whose turn it is, or two different positions collapse into one. The counter
      resets on anything irreversible — a capture, or a placement — and the
      table below drives both rules until they actually fire.
    

    

    

  


  

    
Step 10 — minimax, alpha-beta, and the ply that does not change sides

    

      The search assumes the two players alternate, and in this game they sometimes do not,
      because a mill gives the same player a second move. Written as plain negamax, where
      each level simply negates the level below, that assumption is baked in and captures
      are scored for the wrong side. The fix is to ask whose turn it is at every node
      rather than to infer it from the depth, so a pending removal is just another node
      that happens to maximise again. Alpha-beta then prunes on top of that unchanged, and
      the saving is worth measuring rather than quoting: the table runs the same position
      with pruning on and off and reports both node counts and the identical score.
    

    

    

  


  

    
Step 11 — what a static evaluation is allowed to know

    

      At the bottom of the search there is no time to play on, so the position gets scored
      by counting things. Material dominates, because a piece is worth far more than any
      arrangement. After that come completed mills, lines holding two of your pieces and an
      empty third, the mobility each side has, and the number of enemy pieces that are
      completely blocked. Every term is a difference between the two sides, which is what
      makes the function antisymmetric — scoring a position for White must give
      exactly the negation of scoring it for Black, and the self-check asserts that rather
      than trusting it.
    

    

    

  


  

    
Step 12 — the board has only four different opening moves

    

      Twenty-four empty points look like twenty-four opening choices, and they are not.
      The board's symmetries — four rotations, four reflections, and the less obvious
      swap of the inner and outer squares, which maps the graph onto itself — group
      those points into orbits, and a point's orbit is all that distinguishes it. The
      engine finds the symmetries by searching for every relabelling of the points that
      preserves adjacency, which is honest work rather than an assumed list. It reports a
      group of sixteen maps and four orbits, so the opening tree is four times smaller than
      it looks. That is the kind of fact you want measured, because getting it wrong in
      either direction silently breaks an opening book.
    

    

    

  






  

    
Step 1 — type the lines, compute the edges

    

      Start from the sixteen lines of three. Walk them once and link each consecutive pair,
      and you have the adjacency list with no chance of a typo creating a one-way edge. The
      self-check later asserts symmetry, the edge count, and the degree distribution, so a
      mistake here cannot survive to the game.
    

    

  


  

    
Step 2 — an index from point to line, then mill detection is two lookups

    

      A piece can only complete a mill on a line through the point it just landed on.
      Precompute, for each point, the indices of the lines passing through it, and the test
      becomes: for each of those two lines, are all three points mine. This runs on every
      move in the search, so cheap matters.
    

    

  


  

    
Step 3 — one move generator, three phases, plus removals

    

      The generator asks four questions in order. Is a removal pending, in which case the
      legal moves are removals. Are there pieces still in hand, in which case every empty
      point is a placement. Does this side have exactly three pieces, in which case every
      piece may go to every empty point. Otherwise slide along adjacency.
    

    

  


  

    
Step 4 — applying a move, and the flag that keeps the turn

    

      apply copies the state rather than mutating it, which is what lets the
      search walk the tree without an undo stack and lets the page keep a history for the
      undo button. It validates instead of trusting: a placement on an occupied point, a
      slide between points that are not adjacent, or a removal of a protected piece all
      throw rather than quietly producing a wrong position.
    

    

  


  

    
Step 5 — settling the position after every single ply

    

      One function decides whether the game just ended, and it runs after every move
      including the removal. It checks reduction to two pieces, then no-legal-move, then
      the two draw rules. Order matters: a player reduced to two pieces usually also has no
      moves, and reporting the more specific reason is what makes the status line useful.
    

    

  


  

    
Step 6 — the evaluation, every term a difference

    

      Material, mills, two-in-a-line threats, mobility and blocked pieces, each weighted
      and each written as mine minus yours. Keeping that shape is what makes the function
      antisymmetric, and antisymmetry is what lets one search routine serve both sides.
    

    

  


  

    
Step 7 — search that asks whose turn it is

    

      The node maximises when the side to move is the side we are searching for, and
      minimises otherwise. That single line is what makes pending removals correct. The
      depth-zero cut is skipped while a removal is pending, so the search never scores a
      position halfway through a capture.
    

    

  


  

    
Step 8 — move ordering, because alpha-beta only pays if you help it

    

      Alpha-beta prunes most when the best move is tried first. Mill-forming moves go to
      the front, and removals are ordered by how much enemy threat they take off the board.
      The ordering is deterministic, so the same position always produces the same move and
      the same node count, which is what makes the numbers on this page reproducible.
