Slitherlink — the puzzle where satisfying every clue, at every vertex, can still leave you with the wrong answer.

Draw one closed loop. The numbers say how many of their four sides it uses.

A Slitherlink grid is a block of cells. Some cells carry a number from 0 to 3. You draw segments along the grid lines, and when you are finished the segments you drew must form one single closed loop — no branches, no loose ends, and no second loop sitting somewhere else on the board. A cell marked 3 must have exactly three of its four sides on that loop. A cell marked 0 must have none. Blank cells say nothing at all.

The trap: every clue right, every corner right, still wrong

The two local rules are easy to check. Every clue counts its own four sides. Every vertex of the lattice must have either 0 segments touching it or exactly 2 — one in, one out — because anything else is a loose end or a junction. Beginners write a solver that enforces exactly those two rules, and it looks finished.

It is not finished. Both rules are local. Nothing in either of them forbids the segments from forming two separate rings. Below is every assignment on a 3×3 board that satisfies every clue and leaves every vertex at degree 0 or 2, split by how many loops it actually drew.

How hard is a board, by the clues you are given

Every 2×2 board, over every way of filling its four cells with 0 1 2 3 or a blank. 625 boards in all. The table counts how many have no solution, exactly one, or more than one — a real puzzle needs exactly one.

Why degree 0 or 2 is necessary but nowhere near sufficient

Think of the drawn segments as the edges of a graph whose vertices are the lattice points. A closed loop visits each of its vertices exactly once and leaves again, so each vertex it touches has degree 2, and each vertex it misses has degree 0. That is the necessary half, and it is the half that is easy to test.

The converse fails. A graph in which every vertex has degree 0 or 2 is a disjoint union of cycles — it may be one cycle, or two, or five. Slitherlink demands exactly one. That last condition is global: no amount of looking at one cell or one corner can see it. You have to walk the edges and count components.

What the counting looks like

Each row is an edge assignment on the 3×3 board above. The first two columns are the local tests. The third is the global one that the local tests cannot reach.

The solver, and the pruning that makes it finish

The search assigns one edge at a time, on or off. Brute force over a 3×3 board means 224 assignments — sixteen million, and a 4×4 is already 240. Two cheap prunes collapse that.

Close a cell as soon as its last side is decided. Once all four edges of a clued cell have values, its count is final: if it does not match the clue, abandon the branch now rather than at the leaf. Close a vertex the same way. Once every edge touching a vertex is decided, its degree is final and must be 0 or 2. Both tests fire deep inside the tree, long before a full assignment exists.

Counting the loops

Once an assignment survives both local tests, the on-edges form a disjoint union of cycles, so counting loops is a plain walk: pick any vertex with degree 2 that you have not visited, follow its edges until you return, and mark everything you saw. Repeat. The number of walks is the number of loops. Exactly one, and at least one edge drawn, is a solution.
