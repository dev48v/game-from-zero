TRIANGLE-FREE PICTURES ON SIX DOTS

 

A game with one rule, and the rule decides almost nothing

Draw six dots. Every pair is joined by a line, so there are C(6,2) = 15 lines and C(6,3) = 20 triangles. Two players take turns claiming an unclaimed line — the first player takes red, the second takes blue — and you lose the instant three of your own lines form a triangle. That is Sim, published by Gustavus Simmons in 1969, and it is the whole rule set.

There is no draw, and the reason is the Ramsey number

The first thing to settle is whether the game can peter out with all fifteen lines claimed and nobody beaten. It cannot, and the reason is R(3,3) = 6: any two-colouring of the complete graph on six vertices contains a monochromatic triangle. The PLAY tab does not quote that. It enumerates every one of the 32,768 red/blue colourings of K₆ and counts how many avoid a monochromatic triangle. The answer is 0.

It also runs the same sweep on the smaller boards, and that is where the number becomes interesting rather than merely true. K₅ has 1,024 colourings and exactly 12 of them are triangle-free in both colours, which is why Sim on five dots is a draw. And 12 is not a coincidence: a triangle-free two-colouring of K₅ is a five-cycle in one colour with its complement, also a five-cycle, in the other, so the count has to be the number of Hamiltonian cycles of K₅, which is (5−1)!/2 = 12. The page computes that second number by enumerating permutations, with no colouring anywhere in the argument, and the two counts have to agree.

So Sim only becomes a game at exactly the Ramsey number. On four dots and on five dots it is a draw with best play. On six, somebody has to lose, and the exact solve says it is the player who moves first.

Solving it

A position is a pair of disjoint line-sets, one red and one blue, with neither containing a triangle. There are 1,350,022 of them, and the page counts that twice by methods that share no line of code: once by walking the game forward from the empty board, and once by listing all 5,789 triangle-free subgraphs of K₆ and counting the disjoint pairs whose sizes differ by 0 or 1. Whose turn it is never needs storing — it falls out of the two sizes.

Because a move only ever adds a line, the position graph is acyclic, so there is no search: order every position by how many lines are claimed, walk from the fullest backwards, and each value is one max over its children. A move that completes your own triangle is a leaf worth −1 to you. The root comes out −1 for the first player, and because K₆ is edge-transitive all fifteen openings are the same position up to relabelling — the page checks that by canonicalising each of them over all 6! = 720 vertex permutations and finding 1 class.

Then measure the rule everybody knows

The second player owns a proven win. The question the page is actually about is what happens when they do not play perfectly — when they play instead the one rule every human being who has ever seen this game states out loud: never complete your own triangle.

That rule is not a policy. It is a family of policies, because at a typical position there are 4.56 lines that obey it and the rule says nothing about which one to take. Four members of that family are on the page, all of them legal readings of the same sentence:

keep      among your safe lines, take the one that leaves you the most safe lines
deny      among your safe lines, take one that is still safe for the opponent
the rule  among your safe lines, take any
spend     among your safe lines, take one already poisoned for the opponent

Against a first player who plays perfectly, those four keep the second player’s guaranteed win 46.3993%, 22.6546%, 10.9703% and 0.0813% of the time. That is a 570× spread inside a single rule. The control is a player who has never heard the rule and colours a line uniformly at random, mines included: it keeps 4.5724% — which is 56× better than the worst member of the rule that was supposed to be strictly better than not knowing anything.

And when both sides play it, the game the second player wins with certainty becomes a coin flip: the first player wins 50.3140% of it.

Why the rule is so weak: you cannot attack

Here is the structural fact the rest follows from. A line you claim is your colour. Whether an unclaimed line would complete a blue triangle depends only on which lines are already blue. So a red move cannot make any line more dangerous for blue. Not less dangerous either, except by consuming the line itself.

The page measures that instead of arguing it: over all 6,162,465 non-losing moves in the game graph, the opponent’s count of poisoned lines after your move equals their count before it minus one if you took a poisoned line and minus zero otherwise — 0 exceptions. Your own count, meanwhile, rises on 68.09% of moves, by 0.9176 on average.

There are no threats in Sim. You cannot build one, you cannot bluff one, and nothing you do puts pressure on the other player. The only thing your move does to them is remove one line from the pool you both draw from.

That gives a derived policy for free: if the only lever is denial, spend your moves on lines that are still safe for them and never on lines they were never going to touch. The measurement backs the mechanism — deny at 22.6546% against spend at 0.0813% is a 278× gap between two policies that differ in nothing else.

And then the derived policy loses to a selfish one

🔴 keep — take the safe line that leaves you the most safe lines, and pay no attention whatsoever to the opponent — scores 46.3993% against denial’s 22.6546%. Twice as good, from a rule that ignores the only lever the game has.

It is not a contradiction, but it is a warning about how far a structural argument carries. “Denial is the only interaction” is true. “Therefore maximise denial” is a different claim, and it is false here, because both players run out at once: the line you deny them is a line you have also spent, and with fifteen lines and a first player who must claim eight of them, running out first is the entire losing condition. Counting your own exits is simply a better proxy for that race than counting theirs.

Where the game ends

Under perfect play from both sides the game lasts 13.4371 plies on average and, being a first-player loss, always ends on an odd one — every even bucket in the distribution is exactly zero, over the whole game graph, by construction rather than by luck. It never ends before ply 11, and 23.79% of the time the board fills completely and the first player is left claiming the fifteenth line with nowhere safe to put it.

Being forced like that is rare in general — only 21,054 of the 1,350,022 positions leave the player to move with no safe line at all — and it is lopsided: 14,904 of them have the first player on move against 6,150 for the second, a ratio of 2.42.

One word, and the whole game changes

Sim is a misère game: the player who achieves the pattern loses. Change that single word so that completing your own triangle wins, leave every other rule alone, and the answer inverts — the first player wins, and can force it in 7 plies. The same solver run over the same position graph with the leaf value flipped visits 18.5× fewer nodes to prove it. Avoidance is where all the difficulty lives.

Building it

1. The board is fifteen bits

Number the fifteen lines 0..14 and a colour’s picture is a 15-bit integer. Each of the twenty triangles is a 3-bit mask. Claiming line e completes a triangle inside mask exactly when some triangle t through e satisfies (mask & t) === (t ^ (1<<e)) — the other two edges already there. Four triangles pass through every line, so the test is four comparisons.

function closesTriangle(G, mask, e){
  const L = G.trisOf[e];                       // the 4 triangles through e
  for (let i = 0; i < L.length; i++){
    const t = L[i];
    if ((mask & t) === (t ^ (1 << e))) return true;
  }
  return false;
}

2. A position needs no turn flag

A position is (red, blue), two disjoint masks. Red moves first, so red is on move exactly when the two pictures have the same number of lines. Storing a turn bit would be storing something derivable, and derivable state that is also stored is state that can disagree with itself.

3. Enumerate, then solve backwards

Every move adds a line, so the position graph is a DAG layered by how many lines are claimed. Walk it once from the empty board, keeping a flat list of positions and an open-addressing index from the packed key red*32768 + blue to its slot; build a child table where a move that would complete the mover’s own triangle is stored as -1. Then counting-sort the positions by lines claimed and sweep from the fullest backwards. No recursion, no memo table, no stack.

for (let oi = 0; oi < B.NS; oi++){
  const i = B.order[oi];                       // descending in lines claimed
  let best = -1;
  for (let q = B.off[i]; q < B.off[i+1]; q++){
    const c = B.child[q];
    const x = c < 0 ? term : -v[c];            // term = -1 avoiding, +1 achieving
    if (x > best) best = x;
  }
  v[i] = best;
}

The same table, the same sweep and one flipped leaf value give the achievement variant, which is how the page can say what changing one word does without writing a second engine.

4. Count the positions a second time, from nowhere near the game

The walk says there are 1,350,022 positions, and a walk that is subtly wrong will say so confidently. So the page also lists every triangle-free subgraph of K₆ by brute force over all 32,768 edge subsets, and counts disjoint pairs whose sizes differ by 0 or 1. That argument has no notion of a move, a turn or a game, and it has to land on the same integer.

5. Every rate is exact

A policy here is a map from a position to a set of acceptable moves, played uniformly. That makes the outcome a probability, and a probability over a DAG does not need dice: push a mass of 1 into the root and sweep forward in layer order, splitting each position’s mass evenly over its policy’s candidates and banking it whenever a move completes a triangle. One pass gives the win split and the game-length distribution together, and the three outcome masses have to sum to 1.

The same number is then recomputed backwards, by a value iteration over the same order that never forms a distribution at all, and the two have to agree. A seeded Monte-Carlo replay is thrown at both, and it has to land inside its own standard error rather than anywhere plausible.

6. Play the definition, do not trust the table

The strongest check on a solved game is to play it. The page starts from thousands of positions the table calls won, has the side to move play a winning line and the other side play anything at all, and asserts three things every ply: the winner never completes a triangle, the position after a winning line is always lost for the opponent, and the position after any reply is won again. A table with one wrong entry cannot survive that.
