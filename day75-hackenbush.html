GAMEFROMZERO · DAY 75

🌱 Blue-Red Hackenbush — the first partizan game in this series, where the two players do not share a move set, so the nimbers that solved Nim and Kayles cannot exist. A position is worth a number instead, an exact dyadic rational, and that number answers three questions at once: its sign says who wins, its size says by how much, and “move to keep it as large as you can” is a winning strategy. All three are checked rather than quoted: the sign called every one of 90,567 exhaustively solved pictures — 0 wrong — and playing for the number won 44,974 of 44,974 won positions across four seeds. What a reasonable person does instead is count the edges, and on a single stalk that is exactly a coin flip: it misses on exactly 2n−1 of the 2n stalks of every even length, 0.500000 at length 2 and 0.500000 at length 20, and it never improves. The bottom edge is the whole answer on one stalk — 0 exceptions in 2,097,150 — so a single red edge at the ground with nineteen blue above it leaves Blue ahead by 18 edges and losing by 1/524288. 🔴 And the instinct that feels strongest is the worst thing you can do: chopping the enemy off at the ground loses 39.09% of won positions, 2.08× worse than moving at random (18.78%), while “take down the most edges” loses 29.51% — also worse than not thinking. 🔴 Then recolour the same 90,567 pictures green, so either player may cut any edge, and who moves first goes from deciding 0.00% of them to deciding 95.78%. That is not a near miss: blue-red produces no first-player wins and green produces no decided-in-advance ones, on the same drawings, by construction. Pure vanilla JS, one file, no build step.

THE MARGIN IS USUALLY SMALLER THAN A MOVE

When the two players have different moves, the answer stops being a nimber and becomes a number

Draw some line segments on the ground. Colour each one blue or red. Left owns the blue edges, Right owns the red ones. On your turn you delete one edge of your own colour, and anything that is no longer connected to the ground falls off with it. Whoever cannot move loses. That is the whole game.

Two games in this series have already been solved by the Sprague–Grundy theory — Nim on Day 55, Kayles on Day 70. Both are impartial: whatever moves are available, they are available to both players, so the only thing a position can be is a heap of some size, and the only question a heap answers is does the player to move win? Every impartial position is either an N-position (the player to move wins) or a P-position (the previous player does). There is no third possibility, because the two players are interchangeable.

Hackenbush breaks that in the first sentence. Blue edges are Left's and red edges are Right's, so the players are not interchangeable, and a position can perfectly well be a win for Left whoever moves. The game is partizan, and a nimber has nowhere to live: a nimber is a statement about a game that reads the same from both sides.

What replaces it

A Blue-Red Hackenbush position is worth a number — specifically a dyadic rational, a fraction whose denominator is a power of two. The number is not a score anybody keeps. It is a measurement of the position in units of free moves: a value of +2 means Left is two moves ahead, −1/2 means Right is half a move ahead, and 0 means neither is, so whoever has to move first runs out first.

Four outcome classes exist in general — L (Left wins whoever starts), R, P (the second player wins), N (the first player wins). Numbers only ever produce three of them:

value > 0   Left wins,  whoever moves first
value < 0   Right wins, whoever moves first
value = 0   the SECOND player wins
value ...   N never happens: Blue-Red Hackenbush has no first-player wins at all

The PLAY tab checks that by exhaustion rather than citing it. Every picture with up to eleven edges is solved by a recursion that knows nothing about numbers — it just asks “can I move to a position where you are stuck?” — and the count of N-positions comes back zero. Then the same drawings are recoloured green, so either player may cut any edge, and the count of L- and R-positions comes back zero while N covers 95.78% of them. The two colourings partition the same shapes into non-overlapping outcome classes. Everything Day 55 and Day 70 built answers a question this game never asks.

The value of one stalk

A stalk is a single chain of edges standing on the ground, read from the bottom up. Its value has a closed form, and the form is a binary expansion:

the leading run of k edges of one colour   is worth   +k  (blue)  or  -k  (red)
every edge after the first colour change  is worth   half its predecessor, signed

  B      =  1            BR     =  1 - 1/2         =  1/2
  BB     =  2            BRR    =  1 - 1/2 - 1/4   =  1/4
  BBR    =  2 - 1/2      BRB    =  1 - 1/2 + 1/4   =  3/4
              = 1 1/2                  RB     = -1 + 1/2        = -1/2

Read that again and the headline falls out of it. The leading run is worth at least a whole edge. Everything above the first colour change is a binary fraction strictly inside (−1, 1). So the tail can never reach the leading run, which means the edge touching the ground decides a single stalk on its own — not usually, always. The page sweeps all 2,097,150 stalks up to length 20 and finds 0 exceptions, and separately checks the bound the argument rests on (|value − leading run| < 1) with 0 violations.

That is also why the natural surrogate fails so badly. Counting edges — blue minus red — is what everyone reaches for, and it disagrees with the bottom edge on exactly half the stalks of every even length. Not approximately half: on stalks of length n even, exactly 2^(n-1) of 2^n, because the surplus is 1 + S with S a sum of n−1 steps of ±1, and with n−1 odd that sum is never zero, so it is negative exactly half the time. On odd lengths the failure rate is 2^(n-1) − C(n−1, (n−1)/2), climbing toward a half and never reaching it. It does not improve with size. It is a coin.

Where the definition actually lives

The closed form is a shortcut, and shortcuts are exactly the kind of thing that can be self-consistent and wrong, so the page also computes the value the way it is defined. A game is written { Left's options | Right's options }, and when all of those options are numbers, the game is the simplest number strictly between the best thing Left can move to and the best thing Right can — where “simplest” means born earliest: zero first, then the integers nearest it, then halves, then quarters. Every option of a stalk is one of its own prefixes, so that recursion is short. The two routes agree on all 32,766 stalks up to length 14 on the page and all 131,070 up to length 16 in the verifier.

And that the whole picture is worth the sum of its stalks is a theorem, not a definition. So the page runs the simplicity rule on entire pictures too — 13,441 of them, agreeing with the sum every time — along with the two identities that would break first if addition were wrong: flipping every colour negates the value and swaps L with R, and a picture standing beside its own mirror image is worth exactly zero and is a second-player win.

The number is not only a verdict. It is the move rule.

A verdict is cheap. The stronger claim is that the number tells you what to play: from a position you are winning, move to whichever position leaves the number as large as you can, and you never lose. The page tests that against the exhaustive solver on all 44,974 pictures Left wins — and loses none of them, on every seed.

The controls are the interesting part. Five other policies play the same positions, with ties broken by a seeded generator rather than by whatever order the move list happened to come out in — an arbitrary tie-break moved one of these rates by five points, and a control whose number depends on array order is not a control.

play for the number         loses   0.00%   of positions it was handed as won
by the edge count                   6.50%
cut the tallest enemy stalk        11.42%
MOVE AT RANDOM                     18.78%
take down the most edges           29.51%
cut it off at the ground           39.09%   <- the move that feels strongest

Two of the five heuristics are worse than not thinking. The most satisfying move in the whole game — find an enemy edge touching the ground, cut it, watch the entire stalk fall over — throws away 39.09% of won positions, 2.08× the loss rate of moving at random. It is not close, and it is not noise: four seeds land inside 0.2 of a point of each other.

The reason is the same bound as before. Cutting a red edge low down destroys a lot of edges and almost no value, because the edges you knock down were the ones worth 1/64. Meanwhile the red edge at the ground you just removed was worth a whole unit to your opponent — and you handed it back by removing the blue structure that was standing on it. Size on the screen and size in the game are unrelated quantities, and every heuristic here except the edge count is measuring the screen.

What the count costs, at its worst

The extreme case is a single stalk: one red edge on the ground with nineteen blue edges piled on top. Blue owns 19 of the 20 edges, is ahead by 18, and the position is worth −1/524288. Red wins it, whoever moves. The mirror image, B then nineteen reds, is worth +1/524288 — a win by a margin half a million times smaller than a single edge, which no amount of counting, weighing or eyeballing will ever see. Across all 90,567 pictures, 25.86% are won by strictly less than one whole edge.

What is not here

Blue-Red only. Add green edges that either player may cut and positions stop being numbers — you get switches and infinitesimals, values like * and ↑, temperature, and the whole apparatus of thermography. The green sweep on the PLAY tab is the pure-green case only, which collapses back to Nim; the interesting mixed case is a different day. Trees and general graphs are also missing: a Blue-Red tree still has a number, found by the fusion and convergence rules, and a general graph needs Berlekamp's fusion principle. Stalks are enough to make every point on this page, and they are the only shape small enough to enumerate exhaustively.

Building it

1. A stalk is an integer

Read from the ground up, with the length carried by a leading one-bit, so a prefix — which is exactly what a cut leaves behind — is one mask-and.

code = (1 << len) | mask       // bit i set = edge i is blue
prefix(code, i) = (1 << i) | (code & ((1 << i) - 1))
lenOf(code) = 31 - Math.clz32(code)

2. Two number systems, on purpose

The authority is an exact dyadic rational held in a BigInt numerator over a power-of-two denominator, because 1/524288 is a win and no tolerance is allowed to decide it. Alongside it runs the same arithmetic as value × 2^24 in a plain double, which is exact for every stalk shorter than 24 edges and fast enough to sweep a quarter of a million pictures in a browser. The two are cross-checked rather than assumed to agree.

3. The closed form

leading run of k edges of colour c   ->   +k or -k
each edge after the first change     ->   half the previous one, signed by colour

4. The definition, as a second opinion

Same value, computed as the simplest number strictly between the best Left option and the best Right one. This shares no line with the closed form, and it is what catches a shortcut that is merely plausible.

simplestBetween(L, R):
  for e = 0, 1, 2, ...
    lo = floor(L * 2^e) + 1 ; hi = ceil(R * 2^e) - 1
    if lo <= hi: return (the one nearest zero) / 2^e

5. The oracle that knows no arithmetic

Nothing above is allowed to decide who wins. That comes from a plain win/loss recursion over positions, memoised on the sorted stalk codes, with the four outcome classes read off two queries: does Left win moving first, and does Right.

6. Enumerating pictures, not lists

A picture does not care what order its stalks are in, so positions are enumerated as multisets — non-decreasing sequences of stalk codes summing to the edge budget. Enumerating ordered lists instead would count the same drawing many times and quietly reweight every percentage on the page.

7. Controls play, they do not just predict

Each heuristic is a scoring function over moves; ties are broken by the seeded generator, and every rate is reported as a mean over four seeds with its range. That was not the first version: the first version broke ties by array order, and one policy's loss rate moved by five points when the move generator started appending prefixes at the end of the list instead of in place.

8. The two sweeps that fund the page

stalks   : all 2^21 - 2 stalks to length 20, walked as a DFS carrying value and edge count
pictures : every multiset of stalks totalling 1..11 edges, each solved exhaustively
           then re-answered by the value, by the edge count, by who owns the ground,
           and recoloured green

9. Three assumptions of mine that the engine refused

// 3. "the aggregate count-failure rate is a rate" - it is two rates: odd-sized pictures cannot

10. What this engine is not

// Stalks only. Trees need fusion and convergence; general graphs need Berlekamp's fusion.
// Blue and red only. One green edge and the value stops being a number - you get *, up,
// switches, temperature, and none of that is here.
// No canonical forms, no simplification of general games: every value on this page is a
// NUMBER, which is the one case where the whole theory collapses to school arithmetic.
