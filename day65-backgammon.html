GAMEFROMZERO · DAY 65

🎲 Backgammon — the first game in this series whose search tree has chance nodes in it. Every game so far searched positions you control; here the opponent is a probability distribution, so minimax does not apply and expectiminimax does: at a chance node you take the weighted average over all 21 distinct rolls, six doubles at 1/36 and fifteen ordered pairs at 2/36. This page implements the real rulebook, including the clause almost every hobby implementation silently drops — you must play both dice if any legal order plays both, and if only one can be played you must play the larger, which is not a local check but a small search over move orderings. It also implements the doubling cube and derives the 25% take point rather than quoting it: a cubeless equity is rolled out here, in your browser, and compared against the take/drop decision. Pip counts, bar re-entry, exact-or-higher bearing off, and a conservation invariant asserted after every single move. The expectiminimax recursion is implemented and verified — its value is bounded by the best and worst single-roll outcomes it averages over — while the on-page AI plays at depth 1 so a move returns instantly. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, offline.

Minimax assumes an adversary. Backgammon has a die.


      
Sixty-four games into this series, every search has been the same shape: I move, you move,
      each of us picking the best available. Chess, Checkers, Hex, Quoridor — all minimax or a
      rollout, all over positions somebody chose.


      
Backgammon breaks that, because between my move and yours there is a die. The tree now has
      three kinds of node: max (me), min (opponent), and
      chance — and a chance node has no best child. It has an expected value:


      
value(chance) = Σ  P(roll) × value(best move for that roll)


      
That is expectiminimax, and the branching factor is brutal: 21 rolls × roughly 15 legal
      move sequences each is ~300 children per ply, before you have looked at anything.


      
21 rolls, and they are not equally likely


      
A pair of dice has 36 outcomes. But (3,5) and (5,3) let you play exactly the same moves, so
      the game only distinguishes 21. Six of those are doubles, which arise one way
      each, and fifteen are mixed, which arise two ways each:


      
6 × 1/36  +  15 × 2/36  =  6/36 + 30/36  =  1


      
Treat those 21 as equally likely — an easy mistake, and a tempting one because the loop is
      simpler — and you have overweighted doubles by a factor of two. Doubles give you
      four moves, so the error does not cancel out anywhere; it makes the engine
      systematically over-optimistic about big rolls.


      
Averaging the best is not the best of the average


      
There is a shortcut people reach for: instead of evaluating all 21 rolls, evaluate the
      "average roll" once. It is much faster and it is wrong, by Jensen's inequality — the maximum
      of an average is not the average of maxima:


      
E[ maxm f(m, roll) ]  ≥  maxm E[ f(m, roll) ]


      
The gap between those two numbers is exactly the value of being able to react to
      the roll, which is most of what skill is in this game. The page measures the gap rather than
      asserting it.


      
The rule almost every implementation drops


      
Here is the clause that separates a real backgammon engine from a plausible one:


      

        
You must play both dice if any ordering plays both. If only one
        can be played, you must play the larger.

      


      
Both halves are non-local. You cannot decide legality by looking at one move — you have to
      generate the whole sequence and compare it against every other sequence. A position where
      playing the 5 first blocks the 3, but playing the 3 first leaves the 5 available, is
      illegal to start with the 5, and nothing about the 5 by itself tells you that.


      
So move generation is a small search over orderings, filtered by a maximum. Skip it and
      your engine will happily make moves that no tournament would allow, in exactly the cramped
      positions where the rule matters most.


      
The doubling cube is where the maths gets sharp


      
The cube turns backgammon from a game into a sequence of decision-theory problems. Your
      opponent offers to double the stake. You may take, or drop and concede one point.


      
Drop, and you lose exactly 1. Take, and you play for 2 with winning probability
      p:


      
equity(drop) = −1
equity(take) = 2p − 2(1−p) = 4p − 2

take is better when   4p − 2 > −1   ⟺   p > 0.25


      
25%. Everyone quotes it; this page derives it, then goes further and
      measures it — the take/drop boundary is located by bisection on a real rollout of a
      pure race, and lands where the algebra says it should.


      
The caveat matters and is on the page too: that derivation is cubeless. It assumes you
      never get to double back. Owning the cube is worth something, which is why the practical take
      point sits below 25% — the algebra gives you the floor, not the answer.


      
Why this game produced the first great neural engine


      
TD-Gammon (Tesauro, 1992) learned by self-play and reached world class, years before that
      worked anywhere else. The reason is structural, and it is the chance node again: the dice
      inject exploration for free. A deterministic self-play loop can converge to a narrow band of
      positions it keeps replaying; the dice make that impossible, so the network sees a
      genuinely varied distribution of positions without any exploration machinery at all.


    

  






  

    


      
🔨 How it is built


      
1. One signed array is the whole board

      
// 26 slots: 0 = white bar, 1..24 = points, 25 = black bar.
// Positive = white checkers, negative = black. Sign IS ownership.
const START = [0, -2,0,0,0,0,5,  0,3,0,0,0,-5,  5,0,0,0,-3,0,  -5,0,0,0,0,2, 0];

      
Sign carrying ownership means "is this point open to me?" is one comparison rather than a
      pair of arrays that can disagree:

      
const canLand = (b, to, me) => me * b[to] >= -1;   // mine, empty, or a lone blot

      
-1 is the blot — exactly one enemy checker, which you hit and send to the bar.
      Two or more and the point is made against you. That single inequality is the entire blocking
      rule.


      
2. Move generation is a search, because of the maximum-use rule

      
function legalSequences(board, me, dice){
    const out = [];
    (function walk(b, left, seq){
      let extended = false;
      for (let i = 0; i < left.length; i++){
        if (i > 0 && left[i] === left[i-1]) continue;      // same die, same subtree
        for (const mv of singleMoves(b, me, left[i])){
          extended = true;
          walk(applyMove(b, mv, me), left.filter((_,j) => j !== i), seq.concat([mv]));
        }
      }
      if (!extended) out.push(seq);                          // dead end: record it
    })(board, dice, []);

    const most = Math.max(...out.map(s => s.length));
    let keep = out.filter(s => s.length === most);
    if (most === 1 && dice[0] !== dice[1]){                  // must play the LARGER
      const big = Math.max(...dice);
      const withBig = keep.filter(s => s[0].die === big);
      if (withBig.length) keep = withBig;
    }
    return keep;
}

      
The two filters at the bottom are the rule. Without them the engine is playing a game with
      the same pieces and different laws — and it will look completely normal until a cramped
      position arrives.


      
3. The bar is not a special case, it is a precondition

      
function singleMoves(b, me, die){
  const bar = me === WHITE ? 0 : 25;
  if (b[bar] * me > 0) {                    // ANY checker on the bar
    const to = me === WHITE ? 25 - die : die;
    return canLand(b, to, me) ? [{ from: bar, to, die }] : [];
  }
  ...                                        // only now, ordinary moves
}

      
Early return, not a filter afterwards. "You must enter before you do anything else" is a
      guard on the generator, so no code downstream can forget it.


      
4. Bearing off: exact, or higher from the highest

      
if (allHome(b, me)){
  const exact = me === WHITE ? die : 25 - die;
  if (b[exact] * me > 0) moves.push({ from: exact, to: OFF, die });
  else if (die > highestOccupied(b, me))    // overshoot allowed ONLY from the highest
    moves.push({ from: highestOccupied(b, me), to: OFF, die });
}

      
The second branch is the one that gets written wrong. A 6 does not bear off a checker on
      point 4 while a checker still sits on point 5 — you must play the 5 first. Guarding on "is
      this the highest occupied point" is the whole rule, and it is why bearing off has any skill
      in it at all.


      
5. Expectiminimax, with the weights right

      
const ROLLS = [];
for (let a = 1; a <= 6; a++)
  for (let b = a; b <= 6; b++)
    ROLLS.push({ dice: a === b ? [a,a,a,a] : [a,b], p: a === b ? 1/36 : 2/36 });
// 21 entries, probabilities summing to exactly 1

function chanceValue(board, me, depth){
  let total = 0;
  for (const r of ROLLS){
    let best = -Infinity;
    for (const seq of legalSequences(board, me, r.dice))
      best = Math.max(best, -chanceValue(applyAll(board, seq, me), -me, depth - 1));
    total += r.p * (best === -Infinity ? -chanceValue(board, -me, depth - 1) : best);
  }
  return total;
}

      
a = 1; b = a — the inner loop starting at a rather than 1 is what
      produces 21 instead of 36, and the p field is what stops them being uniform. Two
      characters and one property, and getting either wrong silently biases every evaluation.

      
The best === -Infinity branch is a real case, not defensive padding: a player
      with checkers on the bar against a closed board has no legal move at all and simply
      forfeits the turn.


      
6. What the tests actually assert

      
// conservation, after every move of 400 random games
assert(count(board, WHITE) + off.white === 15);
assert(count(board, BLACK) + off.black === 15);

// the chance node is a probability distribution
assert(ROLLS.length === 21);
assert(Math.abs(ROLLS.reduce((s,r) => s + r.p, 0) - 1) < 1e-15);

// pip count is monotone: no legal move ever increases your own pips
assert(pip(after, me) <= pip(before, me));

      
That last one is a strong, cheap invariant. Every legal move in backgammon moves a checker
      toward home or off the board, so your pip count can only fall — which catches direction bugs,
      the classic sign error, and bear-off mistakes all at once, on every ply of every random game.
