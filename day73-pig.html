🎲 Pig — roll a die as often as you like, bank when you want, but a 1 wipes the turn. Everyone knows the rule: hold at 20. It is not folklore, it is a theorem — one more roll is worth exactly (20 − k)/6 points, so 20 is where rolling stops paying, and 20 and 21 tie exactly at 8.141795 points a turn. It is also the answer to the wrong question, because the game is scored in wins. This page solves the win game exactly — all 505,000 states — and the folk rule does not survive contact. Of the 10,000 score pairs, the optimal hold point is exactly 20 on 557 of them; the table disagrees with hold at 20 on 116,095 of 495,000 decisions and on 7,613 score pairs. Then the finding that rewrote the page. Score every fixed threshold on wins against the perfect player and the curve is not a hill with 20 on top — it has five local maxima at 16, 19, 25, 33 and 50, which are 100/6, 100/5, 100/4, 100/3 and 100/2. It is a resonance with the target: what matters is how evenly your typical bank divides the 100 you need. So 20 is not even a local maximum — 19 beats it, hill-climbing from 20 walks downhill to 19 and stops there, and the real best fixed rule is hold at 25, at 47.89% against 46.01% — while scoring 1.8% fewer points a turn. Move the target to 60 and the maxima move to 15, 20, 30; the points formula cannot follow, because the goal never appears in it. Then the second inversion: hold at n is not merely the wrong n, it is the wrong shape of rule. On 2,503 score pairs the optimal player banks inside a window of turn totals and rolls again above it — 529 of them where the goal is more than two rolls away — and at 8–83 that window is seven turn totals wide with 34 roll-again states stacked on top of it. And the obvious way to write the solver — one backward pass over the score sums — gets 45.21% of decisions wrong while passing every property anyone checks: 10,000/10,000 values in range, 9,900/9,900 monotone each way, exactly like the true solution. The right answer to the wrong question

Pig fits on a napkin. On your turn you roll a die as many times as you like. Every face except 1 adds to a turn total. Roll a 1 and the turn total is gone and your turn is over. Stop whenever you like and the turn total is added to your score. First to 100 wins.

There is exactly one decision in the game — roll again, or bank — and there is a famous answer: hold at 20. What makes that rule interesting is that it is not a superstition. It is a theorem, and it takes one line. Sitting on a turn total of k, one more roll loses k one time in six and gains the average of 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 — which is 4 — the other five times:

expected change from one more roll = (5/6)(4) - (1/6)(k) = (20 - k)/6

Positive below 20, negative above it, exactly zero at 20. So “hold at 20” is the rule that maximises the points you score per turn, provably, with no computer involved. It has a twin that nobody quotes: because the expression is exactly zero at k = 20, rolling at 20 and then stopping is worth the same as stopping at 20, so hold at 21 scores exactly the same 8.141795 points a turn. The points criterion cannot tell those two rules apart at all.

And that is the crack the whole page goes through. Pig is not scored in points. It is scored in wins, and the two objectives are not the same objective.

Why the win game is a different kind of problem

Write down what you need to know: V(i, j, k), the probability that the player about to act wins, given their score i, the opponent's j, and a turn total k already at risk. Two actions:

roll : (1/6)·(1 - V(j, i, 0)) + (1/6)·Σ V(i, j, k+r)   for r = 2..6
hold : 1 - V(j, i+k, 0)

Look at where those arrows go. Holding sends the game to a strictly larger total score i+j+k, so holding always makes progress. Rolling a 1 sends it to (j, i, 0) — the same total score, with the roles swapped. And (j, i, 0) can roll a 1 straight back to (i, j, 0). The state graph has a cycle.

That single fact rules out everything the earlier days in this series did. There is no ordering of the states in which a backward pass works, because two states depend on each other. A depth limit certifies nothing. A tablebase has nowhere to start. This is a stochastic game whose solution is a fixed point, and the usual advice is to iterate the whole equal-score diagonal until it settles.

The cycle is exactly two states long. On a fixed total score s = i+j, the only unknowns any state reaches are V(i,j,0) and V(j,i,0) — everything else it touches is either a bigger score sum (already solved) or a larger turn total on the same pair. So with the policy held still, every value on that diagonal is an affine function a + b·y of one unknown, and the fixed point is a 2×2 linear solve you can write in one line. No tolerance, no convergence test, no epsilon. Wrap policy iteration around it and the whole 505,000-state game is exact in about 50 ms.

What the table says about hold at 20

The solved table has an opinion about the folk rule, and it is not a kind one. Read off the smallest turn total at which the optimal player banks, for every one of the 10,000 score pairs: it is exactly 20 on 557 of them. It is higher than 20 on 5,548 and lower on 3,895. Counted as decisions rather than positions, hold-at-20 chooses differently from the table on 116,095 of 495,000 states, spread over 7,613 of the 10,000 score pairs.

Some of that is obvious once you see it. If your opponent is on 84 and you are on 0, banking 18 points is pointless — you need a run, so the optimal player simply keeps rolling and takes the 2.2% chance rather than the near-zero one. What is less obvious is the other direction: with a comfortable lead the optimal player banks early, because a bust costs a lead more than it costs a deficit.

The finding that rewrote this page

So the folk rule is wrong in detail. The natural next question is which fixed threshold is least wrong, and the natural expectation is a gentle hill with 20 or a little above it on top. Score every threshold from 1 to 55 against the perfect player — exactly, both seats, no sampling — and the curve is not a hill. It has five local maxima:

local maxima at n = 16, 19, 25, 33, 50
      100/6 = 16.7   100/5 = 20   100/4 = 25   100/3 = 33.3   100/2 = 50

They are the target divided by a whole number. That is not a coincidence and it is not numerology: a threshold of n banks about n + 1.7 when it banks at all, so a rule whose typical bank divides 100 nearly evenly wastes nothing at the finish, while one that overshoots on the last turn spent rolls it did not need. It is a resonance with the goal — and it makes the curve jagged rather than smooth, which is exactly what the points model cannot produce.

20 is not a local maximum. hold 19 scores 46.24% against perfect play and hold 20 scores 46.01%, so the famous rule sits on a downslope. Hill-climb from 20 and you walk downhill to 19 and stop, 1.66 points short of the real best fixed rule — hold 25, at 47.89%. And hold 25 gets there while scoring 1.8% fewer points a turn than hold 20 does. The two objectives are pulling in opposite directions over exactly the range anyone would search.

The control: move the goal

“The maxima are at goal divided by a whole number” is a claim with a test attached. If it is true, changing the goal must move them; if it is pattern-matching on five numbers, changing the goal will not. Solving the game at 50, 60, 80, 100 and 120 gives maxima at 12/17/25, 15/20/30, 20/27/40, 16/19/25/33/50 and 19/23/30/40/60 — the halves, thirds, quarters, fifths and sixths of each target, every time.

Meanwhile look at what the points rule does when the goal changes: nothing. (20 − k)/6 has no goal in it, so it says hold at 20 whether you are racing to 40 or to 1,000. At a target of 60 the best fixed threshold is 30 — half again as large as what the points rule recommends — and the points rule cannot even represent the disagreement.

And it is not a threshold rule at all

There is a deeper problem with “hold at n” than the value of n: the optimal policy is not that shape. On 2,503 of the 10,000 score pairs the optimal player banks at some turn total and then, at a larger one, rolls again. Part of that is the endgame — with the goal inside one roll you obviously go for it — but 1,074 pairs still do it where a single roll cannot possibly finish the game (7,417 states) and 529 still do it with the goal more than two rolls away. Both action values rise with the turn total — banking more is better, and so is having more at risk when you are behind — so nothing forces them to cross only once.

The clearest case is the score pair 8–83. There the optimal player banks on a turn total of 45 to 51 and nowhere else: below 45 a bank is too small to matter against an opponent on 83, and above 51 the win is close enough to chase. A seven-wide window with 44 roll states under it and 40 above. No fixed threshold, tuned however you like, can express that shape.

The other way to get this wrong: the solver

The cycle is easy to miss. Sort the states by total score, walk downwards, and when the roll-a-1 branch asks for V(j,i,0) — same total score, not computed yet — put in a constant and move on. One clean backward pass, no fixed point, no iteration. It produces a table that looks right, and here is what that table passes:

  • every one of its 10,000 values lies in [0, 1] — 10,000/10,000;
  • the win probability rises with your own score — 9,900/9,900;
  • and falls with the opponent's — 9,900/9,900.

The correct solution scores exactly the same on all three. Those are the properties a review actually checks, and they cannot see the bug. What the bug does see: the mean value is off by 0.2123, the worst by 0.4929, 223,795 of 495,000 decisions come out different — 45.21% — and put on the table against the real solver it wins 11.64% of games.

What is actually true about Pig

Under perfect play by both sides the first player wins 53.06% of the time, which is the whole first-move advantage in a game with no board. The perfect player beats hold-at-20 53.99% of the time seat-averaged (57.15% moving first, 50.83% moving second), and beats the best fixed rule, hold-25, 52.11%. And hold at 20 versus hold at 21 — the two rules the points criterion declares exactly tied — is 50.84% to 49.16%. Even where the points model has no opinion, the win model does.

None of that needs a simulation. Every number on this page is a linear-algebra answer, exact to the last float, with Monte-Carlo runs used only as a witness that the algebra describes the game people actually play.

🔨 How it is built

1. The state, and the cycle in it

// V(i,j,k): mover's score i, opponent j, turn total k already at risk.
// i + k >= 100 is a win, so V = 1 there and the state does not exist below.
// roll : (1/6)(1 - V(j,i,0)) + (1/6) * sum over r in 2..6 of V(i,j,k+r)
// hold : 1 - V(j, i+k, 0)                          // k >= 1: a turn must roll once
//
// hold  -> score sum i+j+k  > i+j       progress
// roll  -> score sum i+j                a CYCLE: (i,j,0) <-> (j,i,0)

Everything else follows from those four lines. There is no ordering of the states that makes a backward pass legal, which is why this is the first game in the series that cannot be solved by a pass at all.

2. The cycle is two states, so the fixed point is 2×2

// On a fixed score sum, with the policy held still, every value is affine in ONE unknown:
//     V(i,j,k) = a[k] + b[k]*y      where y = V(j,i,0)
// Read the coefficients off the recursion, top of the turn total downwards:
a[k] = 1/6 + (1/6) * sum a[k+r]          b[k] = -1/6 + (1/6) * sum b[k+r]    // roll
a[k] = 1 - V0[j][i+k]                    b[k] = 0                            // hold
// then x = a1 + b1*y and y = a2 + b2*x, so
const den = 1 - b1*b2;
x = (a1 + b1*a2) / den;   y = (a2 + b2*a1) / den;

That is the entire fixed-point solve. No tolerance, no iteration count, no convergence test — and when i === j the same formula collapses to a/(1−b) on its own, because a state can roll a 1 into itself.

3. Policy iteration around the closed form

for (let it = 0; it < 80; it++){
  evalSide(i, j);  evalSide(j, i);          // affine coefficients under the current policy
  solve the 2x2;                            // exact values for this policy
  const changed = improve(i, j) | improve(j, i);   // greedy w.r.t. those values
  if (!changed) break;                      // greedy for its own values == optimal
}

Eleven sweeps is the worst any pair needs, 20,763 in total across 5,050 pairs. Ties are resolved towards roll, which is what keeps the improvement step from oscillating between two equally good actions forever.

4. The exact head-to-head, which is where the numbers come from

// F(i,j,k) = P(A wins | A to move).      H(i,j,k) = P(A wins | B to move).
// Same two-state cycle: F(i,j,0) <-> H(j,i,0). One 2x2 solve per ordered pair, no iteration.
// So "hold 25 beats hold 20 by 1.17 points" is linear algebra, not 10 million games.

Fixing both policies makes the whole thing linear, which is why every duel on this page is an exact probability rather than a sample mean. The forward simulator exists only as a witness: a million games per matchup land within 1.3 standard errors of the algebra.

5. The points side, for contrast

rollEV(k) = (20 - k) / 6;                    // one line, no goal, no opponent, no table
// and the DP that agrees with it:
e[k] = k                       for k >= n
e[k] = (1/6) * sum e[k+r]      for k <  n      // the 1 banks nothing
// turnScore(20) === turnScore(21) === 8.141795...  exactly tied

Note what is missing from that formula: the goal, the opponent's score, and your own. That is the whole reason it cannot see the resonance — a rule that maximises points per turn is optimising a quantity that does not know the game ends.

6. The broken solver, written on purpose

// The obvious approach: one backward pass over the score sums, and when the roll-a-1 branch
// wants V(j,i,0) from the SAME sum - not computed yet - substitute a constant.
let ar = (1/6) * (1 - guess);                // <- the whole bug
for (let r = 2; r <= 6; r++) ar += (i + k + r >= G ? 1 : a[k+r]) / 6;

Every guess in {0, 0.5, 1} yields values inside [0,1] and monotone in both scores, exactly like the true solution, which is why this class of bug survives review. It is only visible if you compare against something, or put it on the table: 11.64% against the real solver.

7. Four things that could have made all of this agree for the wrong reason

// 1. plain Jacobi value iteration over EVERY state, no diagonals, no 2x2  -> 1.2e-14 at goal 30
// 2. policy iteration started from hold-at-1 and hold-at-40 instead of 20 -> identical values
// 3. the Bellman certificate: the stored policy is greedy for its own value, 505,000/505,000
// 4. a forward Monte-Carlo simulator on a different generator                -> within 1.3 sigma

The second is the one that matters most, because policy iteration is the kind of algorithm that can converge to a comfortable local answer. Starting it from a deliberately terrible policy and landing on the same table to the last float is the check that the answer is the fixed point and not the starting guess.

9. Two of my own assumptions were wrong

// 1. "the optimal policy is a hold threshold in the turn total"

// 2. "the win-rate curve is a hill with its peak near 20"
