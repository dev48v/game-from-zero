GAMEFROMZERO · DAY 70

🎳 Kayles — a row of bowling pins, and a ball that knocks down one pin, or two that are side by side. Take the last pin and you win. Knock a pin out of the middle and the row does not shrink, it splits: one game becomes two, and by the middle of a match you are not playing a position at all, you are playing a sum of four or five little games at once. No search can hold that — the branching is the product of the parts. So the engine is not a search. It is the Sprague–Grundy theorem: give every row a single number, its nim-value, and the whole board is the XOR of those numbers. Zero means you lose. Anything else and there is a move to zero. The page computes those numbers by mex from the definition, then proves the decomposition it depends on by brute force over every one of the 65,536 boards of sixteen cells, with a solver that has never heard of a row. Kayles' nim-values never exceed 8 and settle into a cycle of length 12 from row 71 onwards — not observed and extrapolated but certified, by a finite check that the Guy–Smith criterion turns into a proof. And then the part that inverts: change one word of the rules, last pin loses, and the same nim-values still call 96.9% of positions correctly while a bot that trusts them wins 8 of 719 games it was handed as won — and on the one rule set where they are wrong about every single position, the same bot wins 525 of 525, because when every move is winning, being wrong is free. Verdict accuracy over a uniform sample of positions is not a weak proxy for playing strength; it is not a proxy for it at all. Pure vanilla JS, one file, no build step.

One game becomes two, and the search dies

Kayles is the simplest game in this series to explain and the first one whose position is not a position. A rack of pins, a ball, and a move that knocks down one pin or two adjacent ones. Last player to knock a pin down wins. That is the whole rulebook.

Take a rack of thirteen and knock out the pin in the middle. You do not have twelve pins now. You have six and six — two separate games, with no interaction between them beyond the fact that you must move in exactly one of them per turn. Knock a pin out of one of the sixes and you have six, two and three. By ply five a typical position is four or five independent games running in parallel.

Every engine before this one in this series would try to search that. It cannot be done, and not because the tree is deep — because it is wide in a multiplicative way. The number of moves is the sum over parts, the number of continuations is the product over parts, and a rack of twenty-four pins has more distinct reachable positions than there are moves you could ever evaluate. Depth limits do not rescue it either: this game has no evaluation function, only a verdict, and a verdict is not something you can approximate at depth 8.

The theorem that replaces the search

Sprague and Grundy, independently, in 1935 and 1939: every impartial game under normal play is equivalent to a single Nim heap. Not similar to — equivalent, in the sense that you can swap one for the other inside any larger game and nothing changes. The size of that heap is the game's nim-value, and it is computed by a recursion with one line in it:

G(position) = mex { G(p) : p is a position you can move to }

mex S = the smallest non-negative integer NOT in S      mex{} = 0, mex{0,1,3} = 2

And the reason it is worth anything at all: for a sum of independent games, the nim-values combine by XOR. That is the whole theorem. A board of rows 5–3–2 has value G(5) ^ G(3) ^ G(2), and the board is a loss for whoever must move exactly when that XOR is 0.

So the engine is three lines long, and its cost is linear in the length of the longest row rather than exponential in the number of rows. For Kayles the recursion writes itself, because the moves out of a row of n are exactly the splits:

G(n) = mex over all i of  { G(i) ^ G(n-1-i)      knock down one pin, at position i
                            G(i) ^ G(n-2-i) }   knock down two, at positions i, i+1

Why the XOR, and not something friendlier

The temptation is to add. Two rows of two ought to be worth as much as one row of four — same pins, same ball. Measured: G(4) = 1 and G(2) ^ G(2) = 0. Four pins in a line is a win for the player to move; the same four pins split into two pairs is a loss. Pin count is not a quantity this game respects, and neither is parity — over every position up to 24 pins, an even pin total is a loss 17.2% of the time and an odd total 15.2% of the time, which is as close to no information as a statistic gets.

XOR is not a convenient encoding, it is forced. If the board's value were v, the opponent could mirror you into a heap of size v and the analysis of Nim applies verbatim: a sum is a loss precisely when every bit cancels, because any single move changes exactly one part and therefore cannot leave the total unchanged, while any nonzero total has a part whose value shares that total's leading bit and can be reduced to cancel it.

The decomposition is the assumption, so the page attacks it

Everything above rests on one unproved-in-code claim: that the board really is the sum of its runs. That claim is where a Sprague–Grundy implementation goes wrong, and it goes wrong silently — you get plausible numbers and a bot that plays well until it does not.

So the page carries a second solver that does not believe in rows. It treats a board of sixteen cells as a 16-bit mask, enumerates all 65,536 of them, and computes a nim-value for each one by mex over its own successors — no runs, no splitting, no XOR anywhere in it. Then every single mask is compared against the XOR of its runs. 65,536 agreements, 0 disagreements, in about sixty milliseconds, and the same again for each of the four rule sets. That is the Sprague–Grundy theorem, verified rather than cited, on every board a sixteen-pin rack can ever be in.

The sequence settles, and the settling can be proved

The nim-values of a single Kayles row start 0 1 2 3 1 4 3 2 1 4 2 6 4 1 2 7 1 4 3 2 … and never exceed 8. From n = 71 they repeat with period 12, forever. Sixteen earlier terms break that period, the last of them at n = 70, which is why the pattern is invisible in the first few dozen values everybody prints.

Observing a period is not proving one. The proof is a real theorem about octal games (Guy–Smith, 1956): if the largest legal removal is d, and the values satisfy G(n+p) = G(n) for every n from s up to 2s + p + d, then they satisfy it for every n ≥ s. The reason is that computing G(n) for n > 2s + p + d only ever consults pairs G(i) ^ G(n−c−i) in which at least one index already sits in the periodic tail, so the whole option set repeats with it.

For Kayles that turns "check forever" into "check n ≤ 156", and the page does the check and prints the bound. It also carries the negative control that makes the check mean something: claim the period starts one term earlier, at 70, and the same criterion must reject it — which it does, because G(70) ≠ G(82).

Three neighbours, and what changes

Kayles is the octal game 0.77. The dropdown carries three relatives, each one rule away, and they behave nothing like each other:

  • Dawson's Kayles (0.07) — you must knock down exactly two adjacent pins. Period 34 from n = 53, values up to 9. And it has losing single rows, which Kayles does not: 1, 5, 9, 15, 21, 25, 29 … all of them odd, measured over 3,000 terms, for a reason you can see without any theory — see below.
  • Odds only (0.707) — one pin or three, never two. The nim-value is exactly n mod 2, for every n up to 3,000. Every move removes an odd number of pins, so the parity of the whole rack flips every turn and the game is decided before it starts. Sprague–Grundy is correct here and it is also completely unnecessary.
  • Triples (0.777) — one, two or three. This one does not settle. There is no period the page can find, and the largest nim-value keeps climbing as the rows get longer: 20 at n ≤ 100, 39 at 400, 73 at 800, 143 at 1600, 256 at 3000. An eventually periodic sequence is bounded, so a value that keeps growing is not merely unexplained, it is incompatible with the thing everybody assumes about these games.

The mirror trick beats the theory, on exactly one board

Here is a strategy with no mathematics in it at all. Faced with a single row, knock out the middle — the middle pin if the row is odd, the middle two if it is even — and then, forever after, copy whatever your opponent does, on the other side. You cannot lose: every move they have, you have its reflection, so you always have a reply and they run out first.

That is a complete winning strategy for any single row, of any length, and it needs neither mex nor XOR nor the number 71. The page checks it on rows 1 to 200 — 200 wins out of 200 — and it is also the reason Kayles has no losing single row: any row can be halved, because the rule set contains both a 1 and a 2, so one of the two parities is always available. Dawson's Kayles has only the 2, an odd row cannot be halved by removing two, and that is exactly where its losing rows are.

Then the sweep, and the number that decides what this page says. Over every winning position up to 24 pins, the mirror player finds a winning move 100% of the time on one row — and 20.6% of the time on two. Not "somewhat worse". Worse than picking a legal move blindfolded would be if the position told you nothing. Three rows recovers to 59.1% and the overall rate is 52.7%, so the shape of the failure is not "the heuristic degrades with size" either; it is that the pairing idea has an answer only when a pairing move exists, and with two unequal rows there is usually no such move and it invents one.

The moral is not that the mirror trick is bad. It is perfect where it applies, and a rack of pins is exactly where it applies, so a person who has only ever seen Kayles played from a full rack has never seen the theory earn anything. The second row is the whole bill.

Change one word and the numbers stop meaning anything

Misère play: the player who knocks down the last pin loses. Same board, same moves, one word different in the win condition. Sprague–Grundy does not apply — the theorem is a normal-play theorem, and there is no analogue that assigns a single number to a misère game and combines by XOR.

What is striking is how well it nearly works. Solve every position up to 24 pins twice, once under each convention, and the verdicts agree on 96.91% of them. Only 227 of 7,338 positions flip. On that evidence the sensible engineering call is to ship the nim-values and note the edge cases.

So the page measures it instead. Hand a bot the nim-value engine, hand it 719 positions that a perfect misère player wins from, let it move first, and put a perfect misère tablebase on the other side. It wins 8. Not 97% of 719 — 1.1%.

Being right about 96.91% of the state space bought nothing, because the 3.09% it is wrong about is not scattered: it is the endgame. Every game, whatever it does earlier, arrives at rows of one and two pins, and that is precisely where the two conventions come apart. An accuracy figure computed over a uniform sample of positions answers a question nobody was asking; the positions a game actually passes through are not uniformly sampled.

The same measurement across the other rule sets shows how little the 96.91% was ever worth as a summary: Dawson's Kayles flips 39.6% of its positions, Triples flips 0.8%, and Odds-only flips 100% of them — every single position, because the game is pure parity and misère inverts parity exactly.

And then the same race on Odds-only inverts the inversion. The nim-values are wrong about every single position in that game, and the bot reading them wins 525 out of 525. Every move there removes an odd number of pins, so the parity of the rack flips whether you understand the game or not: from an even total, every legal move wins, the engine cannot find a move that zeroes the XOR, falls through to its first legal move, and that move is correct.

So the two numbers do not merely fail to track each other, they run in opposite directions: 3.09% wrong on positions → 1.1% of games won; 100% wrong on positions → 100% of games won. Verdict accuracy over a uniform sample of positions is not a weak proxy for playing strength. It is not a proxy for it at all.

What the engine returns

Not a move. A number per row, XORed. If it is nonzero, every move that zeroes it wins and the rest lose, and the page paints them: 27.6% of winning positions have exactly one winning reply and the rest have several, up to nine. If it is zero, every move loses equally and the bot plays the first one, because in a lost position there is nothing to optimise and pretending otherwise is how engines acquire fake personalities.

🔨 How it is built

1. mex, and the only recursion on the page

function mex(set){ let m = 0; while (set.has(m)) m++; return m; }

function grundyRow(allowed, N){
  const G = new Int32Array(N + 1);            // G[0] = 0: no pins, no move, a loss
  for (let n = 1; n <= N; n++){
    const s = new Set();
    for (const c of allowed)                  // c = how many adjacent pins this rule set lets you take
      for (let i = 0; i + c <= n; i++)
        s.add(G[i] ^ G[n - c - i]);           // knocking from the middle SPLITS: two games, XORed
    G[n] = mex(s);
  }
  return G;
}

Nine lines and the game is solved for every rack length at once. The ^ inside the loop is not an optimisation — a move out of a single row generally lands in a sum of two rows, so the option's value is already a XOR before any board exists.

2. A position is a multiset, sorted, and that is its key

const normRows = rows => rows.filter(x => x > 0).sort((a, b) => b - a);
const rowsKey  = rows => rows.join(",");

Rows are interchangeable and empty rows do not exist, so [5,0,3,2], [2,3,5] and [5,3,2] are one position. Skip the normalisation and the tables silently triple in size while the memoisation stops hitting; every exhaustive count on this page would be wrong in the same direction, which is why the position census is asserted against the partition numbers rather than trusted.

3. The engine, in full

const xorOf = (rows, G) => rows.reduce((v, n) => v ^ G[n], 0);

winningReplies(rows, allowed, G)    // every move whose result XORs to 0
  = rowMoves(rows, allowed).filter(m => xorOf(m.res, G) === 0);

That is the whole player. No depth, no evaluation, no time control. A position is won iff the XOR is nonzero, and the winning moves are the ones that zero it.

4. The second solver, which does not believe in rows

function maskSolve(allowed, W){          // EVERY board of W cells, as a bitmask
  const Gm = new Int32Array(1 << W);
  for (let m = 1; m < (1 << W); m++){
    const s = new Set();
    for (const c of allowed){
      const run = (1 << c) - 1;
      for (let i = 0; i + c <= W; i++){
        const blk = run << i;
        if ((m & blk) === blk) s.add(Gm[m ^ blk]);   // no rows, no splitting, no XOR
      }
    }
    Gm[m] = mex(s);
  }
  return Gm;
}
// then, for all 65,536 masks:  Gm[m] === xorOf(runsOfMask(m), G)

This is the check that matters. The row engine is fast because it assumes the board decomposes; the mask engine is slow because it assumes nothing. Where they overlap they must agree on every mask, and they do — 65,536 per rule set at W = 16, 262,144 at W = 18 behind the deep-sweep button.

5. Two independent exact solvers over multisets

normal.set(k, moves.some(m => !normal.get(key(m.res))));            // no nim-values anywhere
misere.set(k, moves.length === 0 ? true                            // can't move => you WIN
                                : moves.some(m => !misere.get(key(m.res))));
direct.set(k, mex(new Set(moves.map(m => direct.get(key(m.res)))))); // Grundy of the SUM, directly

Three passes over the same move graph, in ascending pin count so children are always finished first. normal is the verdict without theory, direct is the nim-value of the whole multiset computed as one game — and the assertion direct === xorOf(rows, G) over all 7,338 positions is Sprague–Grundy again, stated on sums rather than on boards.

6. The periodicity certificate

// Guy-Smith: if G(n+p) === G(n) for every s <= n <= 2s + p + d, it holds for ALL n >= s.
function certifyPeriod(allowed, s, p){
  const d = Math.max.apply(null, allowed);
  const bound = 2 * s + p + d;
  const G = grundyRow(allowed, bound + p + 1);
  for (let n = s; n <= bound; n++) if (G[n + p] !== G[n]) return { ok: false, at: n };
  return { ok: true, bound };
}
// kayles: s=71 p=12 d=2 -> check n <= 156, and s=70 must be REJECTED

The period is found first by the cheap route — for each candidate p, the last index where G(n+p) ≠ G(n), plus one — and then certified. Finding is O(pN); certifying is a proof.

7. The mirror player, written to be fair to it

botMirror: if any move leaves every row length appearing an EVEN number of times, play it
           else halve the longest row  (take c pins from the middle, c chosen so n-c is even)

The first clause is the actual mirror strategy and it is exact whenever it fires — a paired position is a loss for the mover, because every move can be answered in the twin. The second clause is what a person does when no pairing move exists, and it is the one that is 20.6% right on two rows. Writing it any weaker would have made the comparison meaningless.

8. The misère measurement, and why it is a race and not a percentage

// every misere-WON position up to 16 pins, XOR bot to move, perfect misere bot replying
for (const p of positions) if (misere.get(key(p))) {
  ...  playRows(p, allowed, botPerfectNormal, botMisereExact, ctx, /*misere=*/true)
}
// -> 8 wins out of 719, against a table that agrees with the XOR verdict on 96.91% of positions

A control sits next to it: the same 719 starts with the exact misère bot moving first must return 719 out of 719, or the test is measuring a broken tablebase rather than a broken heuristic.

9. Board moves and row moves, kept honest

// the UI knocks pins out of a board; the engine reasons about runs.
// asserted on random boards: boardMoves(cells) and rowMoves(runsOf(cells)) produce the SAME
// multiset of resulting positions, counted with multiplicity collapsed.

The board has coordinates and the engine does not. Two different move generators therefore exist, and the one place a bug could hide is between them, so the self-test regenerates both on random boards and compares the sets of resulting run-multisets.
