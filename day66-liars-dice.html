GAMEFROMZERO · DAY 66

🎲 Liar's Dice — the first game in this series with imperfect information. Backgammon added chance yesterday; the opponent was still a distribution over rolls you could both see. Here you cannot see their dice at all, so the state is not a position — it is a belief, and the only thing you ever learn comes from what your opponent was willing to bid. The engine is exact rather than simulated: the probability that at least k of a face exist among n unseen dice is a binomial tail computed in closed form, with ones wild, so p = 1/3 and not 1/6 — and getting that single number wrong is the difference between a player that bluffs and one that only ever calls. It also implements the rule people forget: with ones wild, a bid on ones needs its own comparison rule, because one 1 is worth two of anything else. Calling is decided by comparing P(bid is true) against a threshold, and the page shows the whole distribution the AI is reasoning over — plus the inference nobody implements: your own dice change the odds, and the AI conditions on its hand rather than on the table. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, offline.

Sixty-five games of perfect information, and then this


      
Every game in this series so far has shown you the whole board. Chess, Hex, Quoridor — you
      and your opponent see the same position. Backgammon added chance yesterday, but even
      there the dice land face-up: the uncertainty is about the future, not about the present.


      
Liar's Dice removes that. You cannot see your opponent's dice. The state of the game is not
      a position you can evaluate — it is a belief, and the only evidence you ever
      get is what your opponent was willing to bid.


      
The number the whole game runs on


      
A bid says "there are at least k dice showing face f across the table".
      You can see your own hand, so the question is about the n dice you cannot see:


      
P(at least j of face f among n unseen dice)  =  Σ  C(n,i) · p^i · (1−p)^(n−i)
                                              i=j..n


      
And p is 1/3, not 1/6, because ones are wild — a die counts toward face
      f if it shows f or shows a 1. Two of six faces, so 1/3.


      

        
Use 1/6 and every probability on the table is roughly halved. The
        practical effect is not a small mis-estimate — it is a player that never
        bluffs, because every bid above the obvious looks impossible, and calls almost
        immediately. The bug is invisible in the code and obvious in the play.

      


      
The exception is a bid on ones. Ones are not wild for themselves, so there p really
      is 1/6 — and that asymmetry is why the bidding rules for ones are different too.


      
Condition on your hand, not on the table


      
The rule people implement is "how likely is it that 4 fives exist among 10 dice". The rule
      that plays well is:


      
I hold 2 fives already, so the bid of 4 only needs 2 more
from the 5 dice I cannot see — a completely different question.


      
Your own dice are not evidence about the opponent's; they are simply known, so they
      come out of the unknown count entirely. That single subtraction is most of the strength of a
      basic bot, and it is why the AI on this page bids aggressively on faces it is holding.


      
Bidding on ones needs its own comparison


      
A raise must be strictly higher. With ones wild, "higher" is not a simple lexicographic
      comparison of (count, face), because one 1 is worth about two of anything else.
      The standard rule:


      
    
        
  • Switching to ones: you may bid half the current count, rounded up.
    • 
        
  • Switching away from ones: you must bid double the current count, plus one.
    • 
        
  • Within the same category: raise the count, or keep the count and raise the face.
    • 
      


      
Skip those two conversion rules and ones become either useless or unbeatable, depending on
      which direction you got wrong.


      
Calling is a threshold, and the threshold is not 50%


      
The naive rule is "call when the bid is more likely false than true". That is close and it
      is not right, because calling has an asymmetric cost: you also lose the option value of making
      a bid the opponent has to answer. The engine here exposes the threshold as a dial so you can
      watch a bot that calls at 0.5 lose to one that calls at 0.35 — the aggressive player forces
      more decisions, and in an imperfect-information game the player who makes their opponent
      decide more often wins more.


      
What a bid tells you, and why this bot does not use it


      
A strong player treats the opponent's bid as evidence and updates their belief about that
      opponent's hand. That is the whole game at a high level, and it is genuinely hard — you have
      to model an opponent who is modelling you, which is where counterfactual regret minimisation
      and Nash equilibria come in.


      
This engine does not do that, and the page says so rather than implying
      otherwise. It computes exact probabilities conditioned on its own hand and bids against a
      threshold. That is a strong basic player and an honest one — the belief table on screen is
      exactly what it is reasoning over, with nothing hidden behind it.


    

  






  

    


      
🔨 How it is built


      
1. The binomial tail, in closed form

      
function atLeast(n, j, p){
  if (j <= 0) return 1;
  if (j > n) return 0;
  let total = 0;
  for (let i = j; i <= n; i++) total += choose(n, i) * Math.pow(p, i) * Math.pow(1 - p, n - i);
  return total;
}

      
Not a simulation. A sampled estimate would be wrong by a percent or so on every call, and
      those errors compound across a whole game of decisions — so the page computes it exactly and
      then checks it against 200,000 real rolls, which is the right way round.

      
choose is built with a multiplicative loop rather than factorials:
      13! already exceeds 2⁵³ in a way that starts costing precision, and there is no
      reason to go near it.


      
2. Ones are wild, and that is one line with two cases

      
const pFace = face => face === 1 ? 1/6 : 2/6;   // wild counts toward everything but itself

      
Two thirds of the difficulty of this game lives in that ternary. The count in your own hand
      needs the same rule:

      
const countFace = (hand, face) =>
  hand.filter(d => d === face || (face !== 1 && d === 1)).length;


      
3. Condition on your own hand

      
const mine = countFace(myHand, bid.face);
const needed = bid.count - mine;                    // only THIS many from the unseen dice
return atLeast(unseenCount, needed, pFace(bid.face));

      
Three lines, and they are the difference between a bot that plays and a bot that recites
      table odds. If needed <= 0 the bid is already true and the probability is
      exactly 1 — which the function returns rather than computing, because a tail sum from a
      negative index is a silent zero in a lot of implementations.


      
4. The raise rule, including the ones conversion

      
function isRaise(prev, next){
  if (!prev) return true;
  if (prev.face !== 1 && next.face === 1)          // switching TO ones
    return next.count >= Math.ceil(prev.count / 2);
  if (prev.face === 1 && next.face !== 1)          // switching AWAY from ones
    return next.count >= 2 * prev.count + 1;
  return next.count > prev.count ||
         (next.count === prev.count && next.face > prev.face);
}

      
The two middle branches are the ones that get left out. The tests assert both directions and
      the round trip — you cannot convert to ones and back and end up below where you started.


      
5. What the tests actually pin

      
// the closed form against 200,000 real rolls
assert(|atLeast(n, k, 1/3) − simulated(n, k)| < 0.006) for every n, k;

// the wild rule, from the definition
assert(pFace(1) === 1/6 && pFace(4) === 2/6);
assert(countFace([1,4,4], 4) === 3 && countFace([1,4,4], 1) === 1);

// monotonicity, which catches an off-by-one in the tail
assert(atLeast(n, k, p) >= atLeast(n, k+1, p));
assert(atLeast(n, 0, p) === 1 && atLeast(n, n+1, p) === 0);

// and the ordering is a strict partial order - no bid can raise itself
assert(!isRaise(b, b)) for every reachable bid;

      
That last one is cheap and catches the classic >= where a >
      belongs, which would let a player "raise" by repeating the bid forever.


      
6. The honest limit, stated in code

      
// This bot does NOT model the opponent. It computes exact probabilities
// conditioned on its OWN hand and bids against a threshold. A strong player
// would treat the opponent's bid as evidence and update - that is CFR territory
// and it is not what this is.

      
A comment rather than a claim in the copy, so it sits next to the code it describes. The
      belief table on the page is the bot's entire reasoning, printed — if it were doing something
      cleverer, there would be a column for it.
