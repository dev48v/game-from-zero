🎲 Undercut — both players name a number from 1 to 5 at the same time. You score your number, unless you named exactly one less than the other player, in which case you score both. It is the smallest game I know of where the best strategy is provably random, and that is not a hedge or a habit: it is a theorem, and every deterministic rule has an exact price. Nothing here is sampled and nothing is a float. Payoffs are integers, the equilibrium is solved over rationals with BigInt numerator and denominator, and every exploitability figure is a rational compared by cross-multiplication. 🔴 The equilibrium never names 1 and never names 2 — two of your five options carry probability exactly zero, and playing either of them hands an informed opponent 26/9 and 5/9 a round. 🔴 And randomness alone does not save you: rolling a fair die over all five numbers is exploitable for 6/5 a round, worse than three of the five pure strategies it was meant to protect you from. 🔴 Then the part that went against the build — a closed form fits the equilibrium at n = 4, 5 and 6, and breaks at n = 7.

The payoff matrix

1. Every deterministic rule has a price, and it is not small

Exploitability is the most an opponent who knows your strategy can take per round. For a pure strategy it is simply the best reply to it. Every figure below is exact.

The best of the five pure strategies is always say 3, and it still leaks 2 a round. The folk rule — undercut the top, meaning always say 4 — leaks 3. There is no deterministic rule that survives.

2. Randomness is necessary and nowhere near sufficient

“Mix it up” is the advice everyone gives, and it is half of an answer. A fair die over all five numbers is exploitable for — a round — worse than three of the five pure strategies it was supposed to protect you from, because it spends 40% of its weight on the two numbers the equilibrium refuses to name at all.

Read the two rows against each other. Against the equilibrium, three replies are worth exactly 0 and none is worth more — that is what an exploitability of zero looks like from the inside. Against the uniform mixture, saying 4 is worth 6/5.

3. The closed form fits three sizes and then breaks

Solving every board from 2 to 9, a pattern appears immediately. From n = 4 the support is the top three numbers, with weights (n−1, 2, n−2) / (2n−1). It fits n = 4, n = 5 and n = 6 exactly — three consecutive sizes, which is enough to feel certain.

🔴 It fails at n = 7, and not by a small margin: the formula predicts weight on the number 7, and the true equilibrium puts exactly zero there. Adding a seventh option to the game changes the answer not at all — the n = 7 equilibrium is byte-for-byte the n = 6 one. At n = 8 and n = 9 the support widens to five numbers and the denominators go to 123 and 165, with no pattern left to see. Three consecutive fits, and the fourth point refutes it.

How it works

The game is symmetric and zero-sum, so its value is zero and a maximin strategy x on support S satisfies (x⊤A)₃ = 0 for every j in S, with sum(x) = 1, x ≥ 0, and (x⊤A)₃ ≤ 0 everywhere else. Enumerate the supports smallest first, solve each candidate exactly by Gaussian elimination over rationals, and keep the first that satisfies the inequalities. 

function payoff(i, j) {
  let a = i, b = j;
  if (i === j - 1) a = i + j;   // you undercut them
  if (j === i - 1) b = j + i;   // they undercut you
  return a - b;                 // zero-sum: your score minus theirs
}

The rational class is 40 lines and exists so that no claim on this page rests on a tolerance. 4/9 is stored as 4n/9n, and “exploitability is zero” is n === 0n rather than Math.abs(v) < 1e-9.
