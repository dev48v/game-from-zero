GAMEFROMZERO · DAY 72

🍫 Chomp — a chocolate bar with a poisoned corner. A bite takes one square and everything down-and-right of it; whoever is forced to eat the poison loses. Three lines of strategy stealing prove the first player wins every bar bigger than a single square, and the proof computes nothing — it works for bars no machine will ever solve, and it never names the move. This page measures what that costs, exactly. The win really is a needle: of the 184,755 shapes that fit in a 10×10 box only 2,612 are losses for the player to move, a winning bite is 312,237 of 9,053,045 legal bites — 3.45% — and even a won position offers on average 1.71 of them. Then the two things that inverted. First: the single move the proof puts its finger on, eat the far corner square, is a losing move on 80 of the 99 bars up to 10×10, and every one of the 19 it wins has a side of exactly 2 — set the two-row bars aside and the proof's own move wins 3 times in 293. Second: 687 of the 692 bars the deep sweep visits have exactly one winning bite, which makes “the winning bite is unique” irresistible and false. 6×13, 8×10, 9×10, 10×14 and 12×13 each have exactly two, and after 34,020,393 solved positions not one bar has three. The smallest counterexample is 78 squares, not 80 — 6×13 is thinner than 8×10, so a sweep of square-ish bars walks straight past it. What does exist is one closed form — an L loses exactly when its two arms are equal, 0 exceptions — and it is worth almost nothing: it catches 10 of the 2,612 losses and beats “just guess the mover wins” by 0.0054 of a percentage point. Pure vanilla JS, one file, no build step.

THE DEEP SWEEP — 12 BOXES, 692 BARS

Long thin boxes as well as square ones, because a bar can be an exception on square count without being one on side length. This is the run that finds 6×13 — and it takes about a minute.

A proof that wins the argument and tells you nothing

Chomp is one paragraph of rules. A chocolate bar sits in front of you, n squares tall and m wide. The square in the top-left corner is poisoned. On your turn you pick any square that is still there and eat it together with every square down-and-right of it. Whoever is left with nothing but the poison has to eat it, and loses.

Now the famous part. Whatever the bar, as long as it is bigger than that single poisoned square, the first player wins. Here is the whole proof.

Strategy stealing. Consider the tiny bite that takes only the far corner square. Either the position it leaves is a loss for your opponent — in which case play it, you have won — or it is a win for them, meaning they have some reply M that wins from there. But M is a bite you could have taken yourself on move one, and taking it removes the far corner square anyway, so it reaches the very same position with the roles swapped. Either way the first player has a winning opening. ∎

Read that again and notice what is missing. The argument never decides which of the two cases it is in. It works for a 4×5 bar and for a 4,000×5,000 bar that no computer will ever solve. It hands you a certainty about the outcome and zero information about the move. That gap is the subject of this page, and unlike the proof, measuring it requires actually solving the game.

A position is a staircase

Every reachable position is a partition. Because a bite removes a whole down-and-right block, the column heights can never go back up as you move right: the surviving squares always form a staircase h[0] ≥ h[1] ≥ … ≥ h[m-1]. So the state is not “a subset of the squares” — a 10×10 bar has 2100 subsets — it is a partition inside a 10×10 box, and there are exactly C(20,10) = 184,756 of those. That single observation is the difference between impossible and instant.

Better still, the value of a position depends only on its shape, never on the bar it started life in. Every rectangle up to 10×10 is a shape inside the 10×10 box, so one table of 184,756 entries answers all 100 bars at once — and every mid-game position of every one of them.

The table fills itself in one upward pass

Rank each staircase into a dense integer: rank(h) = ∑i CUM[i][h[i]], where CUM[i][v] = C(v + m-i-1, m-i) counts the completions that come before. It is a bijection onto 0 … C(n+m,m)-1, and — this is the useful part — it is a sum of terms each strictly increasing in its own column. A bite only ever shrinks columns, so the position after a bite always has a strictly smaller rank.

That means no recursion, no memo map, no visited set, no stack. Loop idx from 1 upwards; every position a bite can reach has already been decided. The solver is a for loop over a Uint8Array, and the 13×13 box — 10,400,600 positions — fits in ten megabytes and about ten seconds.

How thin the win actually is

With the table in hand the gap left by the proof becomes arithmetic. Inside the 10×10 box there are 184,755 non-empty shapes. Only 2,612 of them — 1.41% — are losses for the player to move. Across all those shapes there are 9,053,045 legal bites and only 312,237 of them win: 3.45%. Being told “you are winning” narrows a real position down to about one bite in twenty-nine.

On the bars themselves it is thinner still. All 99 playable rectangles up to 10×10 are wins, exactly as promised, and 95 of them have precisely one winning bite out of up to 99 legal ones. The theorem is true, complete, and useless at the board.

The move the proof names is usually the wrong one

The strategy-stealing argument does put its finger on one specific bite — the far corner square — and it is tempting to walk away thinking that is the move. It is not. On the 99 bars up to 10×10 the corner bite wins 19 times and loses 80, and every single one of the 19 is a bar with a side of exactly 2, where the corner bite happens to be the “leave the bottom row one longer” move. Widen the search and the pattern barely improves: over the 293 bars in the deep sweep that are not two rows tall, the corner bite wins three times — 6×11, 6×15 and 6×27, which do not look like they belong together and, as far as this engine can tell, do not.

The pattern that begs to be a theorem

Once you see 95 of 99 bars with exactly one winning bite, the next thought is unavoidable: the winning bite in a rectangle must always be unique. The 10×10 box already says otherwise — 8×10 has two, at (row 4, col 5) and (row 3, col 8), and so does 9×10 — and the deep sweep says it properly. Twelve boxes, deliberately including long thin ones, 34,020,393 positions, 692 bars:

  • 687 have exactly one winning bite.
  • 5 have exactly two: 6×13, 8×10, 9×10, 10×14, 12×13.
  • 0 have three or more.

The shape of that result matters as much as the result. The smallest counterexample by square count is 6×13 at 78 squares, not 8×10 at 80 — and 6×13 does not fit in any square box below 13×13. Sweeping n×n bars outward, the obvious thing to do, meets 8×10 first and never learns that something smaller was hiding in a thinner box. That is why the sweep plan runs a 6×30 and a 3×120 and even a 2×400.

Why losing positions are rare, in one sentence

Take any position with k+1 rows and fix its first k. A bite in the last row changes nothing else, so within that family the positions are all one bite from each other, which means at most one of them can be a loss — two losses one bite apart is a contradiction in terms. The 10×10 box has 92,378 such families and the engine checks every one: 0 violations, and only 2,612 families contain a loss at all. The rest is 89,766 families where every last-row length leaves the mover winning.

The one closed form there is

An L — one arm of a squares along the top row, one arm of b down the first column — loses exactly when a = b. Nothing else in Chomp is that clean, and the engine confirms all 100 L's in the box with 0 exceptions. It also explains the one thing everybody remembers about square bars: the winning bite on an n×n is always at (1,1), because that leaves an L with two arms of n-1. The engine agrees for every square from 2×2 to 10×10, and each time it is the only winning bite.

Two rows have a rule too, and it is just as short: a two-row position loses exactly when the bottom row is one square longer than the top. Checked against an independent two-row solver on all 45,450 positions with rows up to 300: 0 exceptions.

Three rows, and the rules stop

The last-row bound applies again: for a three-row position, fixing the second and third rows leaves at most one top row that loses. That holds — 210 families with second row up to 20, not one with two answers. The natural next sentence, “and exactly one always exists”, is where it falls apart: 75 of the 210 families have no losing top row at all, searched out to a top row of 400. Every family whose third row is 1 and whose second row is 3 or more is empty; so is every family with third row 3 and second row from 6 up.

And where an answer does exist it refuses to be a formula. Third row 2 is beautifully behaved — the losing top row is always the second row plus 2, for every second row from 2 to 20. The diagonal, second row = third row, gives 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 11, 13, 15, 16, 18, 20, 21, 24, 25, 27, 28, 30, 32, 34, 35. The gaps run 1, 2, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1 — a jump of 3 in the middle of a sequence that had been alternating politely. One extra row is the entire difference between a rule you can hold in your head and a table you have to compute.

What a guess is worth

Every simple rule anyone reaches for gets scored here on all 184,755 shapes. Counting squares and checking parity — the reflex borrowed from Nim — comes out at 49.97%, which is a coin. The exact rules score high only because losses are rare: guessing “the mover wins” every single time is already right 98.59% of the time, and the best honest rule on the list, the equal-armed L, beats that by 0.0054 percentage points because it recognises 10 losses out of 2,612 without ever raising a false alarm. A rule with no false alarms and no coverage is a rule you cannot play with.

The final measurement is the practical one. Against an opponent biting at random, perfect play wins every game. The corner-bite opener — the proof's move, played as a policy — wins about 54%. And if both sides bite uniformly at random, the exact dynamic program says the first player's chance on a 10×10 bar is 0.500041697. A theorem worth 100% collapses to a coin flip the moment nobody can find the move it promised.

What this engine is not. It is exact and total for any shape that fits in a box it can afford, and the cost is the number of partitions in that box — C(n+m, m), which is combinatorial, not exponential in the area. That is why 13×13 is comfortable and 20×20 (C(40,20) ≈ 137 billion) is not. It solves poisoned-corner Chomp on rectangles and staircases; three-dimensional Chomp, ordinal Chomp on a continuous bar, and Chomp on a general poset each need a different state space, and nothing here widens to cover them. It is also not a Sprague-Grundy story: Chomp does not split into independent sums, so there is nothing to XOR.

🔨 How it is built

1. The state, and why it is small

// h[0] >= h[1] >= ... >= h[M-1] >= 0, each <= N.  Square (r,c) is present iff r < h[c].
// A bite at (r,c) truncates every column from c rightwards down to r:
function biteAt(h, r, c){
  const g = h.slice();
  for (let j = c; j < g.length; j++) if (g[j] > r) g[j] = r;
  return g;
}

Four lines, and they encode the entire rulebook. Note what falls out for free: distinct squares always give distinct positions, because c is the first column that changed and r is its new height. So “number of legal bites” is exactly “number of squares minus one”, with no de-duplication pass.

2. Ranking a staircase into an integer

// CUM[i][v] = C(v + M-i-1, M-i) counts the completions that sort before this column value.
function rankShape(B, h){
  let s = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < B.M; i++) s += B.CUM[i][h[i]];
  return s;
}

A bijection onto 0 … C(N+M,M)-1. The property the whole solver leans on is that each term depends on one column and increases with it, so shrinking any column strictly lowers the rank. Every bite therefore points backwards.

3. The solver is a for-loop

for (let idx = 1; idx < T; idx++){
  unrank(idx into h);
  if (only the poison is left){ win[idx] = 0; continue; }
  let w = 0;
  for each legal bite (r,c)
    if (!win[rank of the position after the bite]){ w = 1; break; }
  win[idx] = w;
}

No recursion, no memo, no hash map, no explicit topological order. The ranking is the topological order. That is why a ten-million-state table costs ten seconds and ten megabytes instead of blowing a call stack.

4. Making a bite cost O(1)

// pre[c]   = rank contribution of the untouched columns to the left
// split[r] = first column already at or below height r  (h is descending, so this is monotone)
// RC[r][j] = prefix of CUM over columns, all taken at height r
// suf[k]   = contribution of the tail that the bite does not reach
const k = split[r] > c ? split[r] : c;
const after = pre[c] + (RC[r][k] - RC[r][c]) + suf[k];

The naive version rebuilds the shape and re-ranks it, which is O(M) per bite and turns the 13×13 box into a minute. This version never materialises the child shape at all: three table lookups and two additions give its rank directly. It is the difference between the deep sweep being a feature and being a promise.

5. A second solver that shares nothing

function bruteWin(h){
  if (h[0] === 1 && (h.length < 2 || h[1] === 0)) return false;
  for (let c = 0; c < h.length; c++)
    for (let r = 0; r < h[c]; r++){
      if (r === 0 && c === 0) continue;
      if (!bruteWin(biteAt(h, r, c))) return true;
    }
  return false;
}

No table, no ranking, no memo — it re-explores the same position thousands of times and does not care. It is unusable past about twenty squares and it is the reason the fast solver can be trusted: the self-test runs both over every shape in a small box and demands zero disagreements.

6. The families bound, as code

// group shapes with k+1 rows by their first k rows; a bite in the last row touches nothing else
const key = rowsOf(h).slice(0, -1).join(".");

Two positions in one group differ only in the last row's length, so the longer one can bite down to the shorter. If both were losses, a loss would be one bite from a loss — impossible. The engine still counts the violations rather than asserting the bound, because a bound you assumed is a bound you never tested.

7. Random play, exactly

p[idx] = 1 - (sum of p[child] over every legal bite) / (number of legal bites);

The same ascending pass, different arithmetic: the exact probability that the player to move wins when both sides bite uniformly at random. No sampling, no seeds, no error bars — and the verifier checks it by doing the opposite, playing hundreds of thousands of actual random games forward and comparing the frequency.

8. The sweep plan

const SWEEP_PLAN = [[2,400],[3,120],[4,64],[5,44],[6,30],[7,23],

Row [n, cap] solves an n × cap box once and reads off every bar n × m for m up to cap. The thin rows are not padding — 6×13 is the smallest bar with two winning bites and it only appears if something goes at least 30 columns wide at six rows tall. A plan of squares alone reports 8×10 as the smallest and is wrong.

9. Three of my own assumptions were wrong

// 1. "the winning bite in a rectangle is unique"        - false, 5 bars in the sweep have two
// 2. "the smallest exception is 8x10"                   - false, 6x13 is 78 squares to its 80
// 3. "every (b,c) has exactly one losing top row"       - false, 75 of 210 families have none

All three survived until the sweep was widened. The first two died because the search was square-shaped; the third because “at most one” is a proof and “exactly one” is a hope. Each is now a self-test that fails loudly if the engine ever drifts back.
