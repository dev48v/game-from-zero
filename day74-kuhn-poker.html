GAMEFROMZERO · DAY 74

🃏 Kuhn poker — three cards, one bet, and the first game in this series where you cannot see the position. With hidden information there is no best move, only a best distribution, and the first player's optimal set is not a point but a segment: bluff the Jack at any rate α ∈ [0, 1/3] as long as you bet the King at exactly 3α and call with the Queen at exactly α + 1/3. Every member is worth exactly −1/18 a hand and is exactly unexploitable — certified in BigInt rationals, not to a tolerance. Now find it the way anyone would, by sweeping a grid over the strategy space, and the answer is decided by arithmetic, not resolution: a grid of step 1/q contains exactly q/3 + 1 unexploitable strategies when 3 divides q and exactly zero otherwise, because every payoff on that grid is a multiple of 1/6q and the game value is not. So 2,197 cells at step 1/12 contain five exact optima and 1,030,301 cells at step 1/100 contain none — 469× the work for a floor of 1/900 that no amount of searching can beat. Refining is not the fix. Every power of two fails, and doubling one improves the floor by exactly 4× or by exactly nothing, alternating forever: 1/32 and 1/64 return the identical floor 1/576, 1/128 and 1/256 the identical 1/2304. Going from 100 steps to 101 halves the floor to 1/1818; going to 102 takes it to zero. All of it is one rounding question — whether 1/3 lands above or below the nearest grid point — because under-calling the Queen costs exactly twice what over-calling costs. Then the two other things a reasonable person does. Hill-climb instead: the 1/30 grid has 31 local minima and 20 of them are not optimal. Or test against a solved opponent: all 29,791 grid strategies score exactly the game value against the equilibrium, and 18 of the 64 pure strategies tie it too — the worst of them leaking 4/9, eight times the value of the whole game. Pure vanilla JS, one file, no build step.

Where the optimum lives, and why a grid cannot see it

Kuhn poker is the smallest poker there is. Three cards — J, Q, K. Each player antes 1 and gets one card, face down. The first player checks or bets 1. If they check, the second player checks (showdown for the pot of 2) or bets 1, and the first player folds or calls. If they bet, the second player folds or calls. Higher card wins the pot. That is the whole game: six possible deals and at most three decisions.

Every previous game in this series had a position you could look at. Here you cannot. You know your own card and nothing else, so you are not choosing a move in a position — you are choosing what to do in an information set, a bundle of positions you cannot tell apart. The first player has six of them (a card, and either the opening decision or the decision after checking into a bet) and so does the second. Six binary decisions each, so 64 pure strategies a side and a 64×64 payoff matrix that fits on a screen.

And that matrix has no saddle point. Not one of its 4,096 cells is both the worst in its row and the best in its column. The best guarantee a pure first-player plan can make is −1/6; the best a pure second-player plan can hold it to is +1/6. There is a third of an ante of daylight between those two numbers, and the value of the game, −1/18, sits inside it. Randomising is worth exactly 1/9 of an ante a hand to the first player, and it is not optional: zero of the 64 pure strategies have zero exploitability, and the median one leaks 4/9.

The optimum is a segment

Two of the six decisions answer themselves. Calling a bet with the Jack loses 2 instead of 1, every time; calling with the King wins 2 instead of folding for −1, every time. Those are checked here by exhaustion rather than asserted — 4,096 opponent pairs each, zero exceptions. What is left is three numbers: how often you bet the Jack (a bluff), how often you bet the King (a value bet), and how often you call with the Queen.

The optimal set is not a point in that cube. It is a straight line:

bet the Jack   with probability  alpha,   alpha in [0, 1/3]
bet the King   with probability  3*alpha
call the Queen with probability  alpha + 1/3

Every point on it is worth exactly −1/18 and has exploitability exactly zero. So never bluffing is optimal — at α = 0 you check every hand you are dealt, including the King, and you cannot be beaten by more than the game already beats you. Always betting the King is optimal too, at α = 1/3, where you bluff the Jack one hand in three. What is not optional is the ratio. Value bets and bluffs are locked together at 3:1, and the page measures what breaking the lock costs:

with the Queen call at alpha + 1/3, the exploitability is exactly
        | bet-King-rate  -  3 * bluff-rate |  /  6

That is an identity, not a fit — 3,721 points checked, worst deviation below 10−15. The other direction has an asymmetry in it that turns out to run the whole page: with the ratio held correct, calling the Queen less often than α + 1/3 by Δ costs Δ/3, and calling more often by Δ costs Δ/6. Under-calling is exactly twice as expensive as over-calling.

Now go and find it

You would not be told the answer. You would sweep the cube: put the three probabilities on a uniform grid of step 1/q, evaluate every cell against every one of the opponent's 64 pure strategies, and keep the cells with zero exploitability. That is a completely reasonable plan, and whether it returns anything at all has nothing to do with how fine the grid is.

Every payoff on a step-1/q grid is a multiple of 1/6q. The expectation is an average over six equally likely deals of terminal payoffs in {±1, ±2} weighted by probabilities that are multiples of 1/q — so it lands in (1/6q)ℤ, always. The game value is −1/18. For that to be a multiple of 1/6q you need 6q/18 = q/3 to be a whole number. So if 3 does not divide q, no cell on the grid can even attain the value, let alone be unexploitable, and the sweep returns nothing — at any resolution, for a reason that has nothing to do with poker.

When 3 does divide q the count is exact: the grid holds q/3 + 1 unexploitable strategies, one for each α = k/q with k = 0…q/3. Swept cell by cell for every q from 2 to 36 and again at 60, 64, 100 and 120, that formula is right every time.

The control: refine the grid

The natural response to “the sweep found nothing” is to make the grid finer. Here is what that buys. The best exploitability available on a failing grid is 1/18q when q ≡ 2 (mod 3) and 1/9q when q ≡ 1, and both come from the same place: the best cell is always “never bluff, never bet the King, call the Queen at the grid point nearest 1/3”, and the nearest grid point is above 1/3 in one residue class and below it in the other. Above costs Δ/6. Below costs Δ/3. Twice as much, for the same distance.

  • 1/12 — 2,197 cells, 5 exact optima.
  • 1/32 — 35,937 cells, none, floor 1/576.
  • 1/64 — 274,625 cells (7.6× the work), none, floor 1/576. Identical. The doubling bought nothing.
  • 1/60 — 226,981 cells, 21 exact optima. Finer than 1/12 and it works, because 60 is divisible by three.
  • 1/100 — 1,030,301 cells, none, floor 1/900.
  • 1/101 — one more grid point, and the floor halves to 1/1818.
  • 1/102 — one more again, and the floor is 0, with 35 exact optima.

Every power of two is in the failing class, because 2k mod 3 alternates 2, 1, 2, 1… and never reaches 0. Worse, the alternation makes the floors pair up exactly: the extra factor of two in the numerator when q ≡ 1 cancels the factor of two in q. So (2, 4) share a floor, (8, 16) share one, (32, 64), (128, 256), (512, 1024), (2048, 4096) — every second doubling of a power-of-two grid improves the best achievable answer by exactly zero, forever.

Control two: hill-climb

If sweeping is too expensive, climb. Start somewhere, step to a neighbour that lowers exploitability, stop when none does. On the 1/30 grid that procedure has 31 places to stop and only 11 of them are optimal — 20 traps, nearly two thirds. The surface is a maximum of 64 linear functions, so it is piecewise linear with plenty of ridges, and nothing about it is convex in the direction a local search walks.

Control three: play it against a solved opponent

The last reasonable idea is to skip the theory and just test: sit your candidate down against a known-optimal opponent and see whether it loses more than −1/18. This has exactly zero power, and not approximately.

Against the equilibrium, all 29,791 strategies on the 1/30 grid score exactly −1/18 — including the 29,780 that are exploitable. On the pure side, 18 of 64 pure strategies tie the equilibrium exactly, and the worst of those leaks 4/9: eight times the entire value of the game, undetectable by the test. This is what an equilibrium is: it makes its opponent indifferent everywhere it can be reached. It does not punish. It declines to lose.

Two things that came out backwards

First, the leaks do not add up. Always betting the King while never bluffing leaks 1/6. Always calling the Queen leaks 1/9. Doing both leaks 1/9 — less than the first mistake alone. Exploitability is a maximum over 64 punishers, not a sum of errors, and a second error can shift which punisher is worst in a way that helps.

Second, and sharper: once you have committed to betting the King every time, the equilibrium's own Queen-calling rate is the worst rate to pair it with. Sweep it and the best answer is 2/3, not 1/3, and it takes the leak from 1/6 to 1/18 — a third of what it was. A component of an optimal strategy is not an optimal component.

And the machine that finds it anyway

Regret matching — counterfactual regret minimisation, run over the full tree with no sampling — converges here, but only in average. The average strategy's exploitability falls steadily; the current iterate's does not fall at all. Over the last half million of a million iterations the current strategy's exploitability oscillates between 0.2778 and 0.6667 and never once drops below a quarter, while the average sits at 5.1×10−4. Returning the last iterate is the single most common way to get this wrong, and the number it returns is roughly 500× worse than the number sitting next to it in memory.

Where does the average land on the segment? At α ≈ 0.208 — interior, neither endpoint, nothing anybody chose. The check that it really is on the segment is that its three coordinates agree: the bluff rate, a third of the King-bet rate, and the Queen-call rate minus 1/3 all read 0.2077…0.2084 at a million iterations. Three numbers derived from three different information sets, agreeing to three decimals, is the evidence — not the exploitability figure, which a bug could also make small.

🔨 How it is built

1. The whole game is one function of six numbers

// p[0..2] = P(bet | J,Q,K)      p[3..5] = P(call | J,Q,K) after checking into a bet
// q[0..2] = P(call | J,Q,K)     q[3..5] = P(bet  | J,Q,K) facing a check
function uDeal(c1, c2, p, q){
  const w2 = c1 > c2 ? 2 : -2, w1 = c1 > c2 ? 1 : -1;      // showdown for 4, and for 2
  const betVal   = (1 - q[c2]) + q[c2] * w2;                // they fold, or they call
  const pc = p[3 + c1];
  const checkVal = (1 - q[3 + c2]) * w1 + q[3 + c2] * ((1 - pc) * (-1) + pc * w2);
  return p[c1] * betVal + (1 - p[c1]) * checkVal;
}
uVal = average of uDeal over the six ordered deals   // no tree object, no recursion

Six deals, three decisions, no search anywhere. Everything else on this page is built out of this one expression.

2. Best response by enumeration, because 64 is small

br2(p) = min over m in 0..63 of uVal(p, pureStrat(m))    // the punisher
exploit1(p) = GAME_VALUE - br2(p).val                   // what the leak costs, per hand

With 64 pure strategies a side there is no need for a counterfactual-value pass: the exact best response is a loop. The verifier computes the same quantity by tree recursion over reach probabilities instead, which is the check that the enumeration is not accidentally right.

3. The certificate is exact, so “optimal” means optimal

// BigInt fractions, reduced by gcd. No tolerance, no epsilon.
class Q { add sub mul cmp isZero }
eqP1(alpha) = [alpha, 0, 3*alpha, 0, alpha + 1/3, 1]
for each alpha in {0, 1/12, 1/9, 1/6, 1/4, 1/3}:
    exploit1 === 0   and   exploit2 === 0   and   u === -1/18     // as rationals

Two exact zeros are a proof of equilibrium, and a proof of equilibrium fixes the value at −1/18 without solving a linear program. The page does not quote Kuhn's 1950 result; it writes the family down and certifies it, and the 4-parameter sweep then re-derives the same line from scratch without being told it exists.

4. The sweep is integer arithmetic, so a zero is a zero

// For each of the 64 pure opponents, 6*u is AFFINE in the three probabilities:
//     6u = A + B x + C y + D z          (A..D integers, checked on 6,912 points)
// Put x = i/q, y = j/q, z = k/q and multiply through by q:
//     6 q u = A q + B i + C j + D k     <- an integer
// exploitability zero  <=>  min over the 64 of that integer  ===  6 q * (-1/18) = -q/3
//     18 q * exploit = -q - 3 * min     <- also an integer, and THAT is the test

No float ever decides whether a cell is optimal. And the same integer says why the whole thing works: -q - 3*min is congruent to -q mod 3, so when q is not a multiple of 3 it cannot be zero, whatever the grid does.

5. The floor of a failing grid, proved rather than searched

// lower bound: 18q*exploit is a positive integer congruent to -q mod 3
//              so it is at least 1 when q % 3 === 2, at least 2 when q % 3 === 1
// upper bound: one witness cell   alpha = 0, betK = 0, callQ = nearest grid point to 1/3
//              which by the leak identity costs 1/(18q) or 1/(9q) exactly
// -> gridLaw(q) returns the floor for ANY q, including 2^20, with no sweep at all

The exhaustive sweeps for q = 2…36 are not how the law is established, they are how it is tested: 0 disagreements. That is what lets the page state the floor at 1/4096 honestly — the claim is arithmetic, and the sweep is the witness that the arithmetic describes the same object.

6. The second seat, and where the bound goes slack

// The same congruence gives the second player a lower bound of 1 (q%3===1) or 2 (q%3===2).
// Measured floor: 4 and 2. So the bound is TIGHT for the first player and 4x SLACK for the
// second, and the resulting floors come out at exactly 2x the first player's, every q.

That factor of two is measured, not derived, and it is stated that way. The second player's optimum is also a single point rather than a segment — call the Queen at 1/3, bet the Jack at 1/3 — so it needs 1/3 to be representable twice, and the sweep finds exactly one cell whenever 3 divides q and zero otherwise.

7. Regret matching, and the two strategies it holds

for t = 1..T:
   current = regretMatching(R)                 // what the iterate is
   accumulate reach-weighted current into S    // what the AVERAGE is
   update R with counterfactual regrets        // full tree, all six deals, no sampling
avgStrategy = S normalised per information set  // this is the thing that converges

Both are kept and both are measured every snapshot, which is the only way the divergence shows up. The exploitability of the average is monotone in the log; the exploitability of the iterate is a permanent oscillation, and its floor over the last half of the run is a bound in the self-test rather than a number, because the oscillation is not periodic.

8. Deterministic randomness

// mulberry32, seeded from a counter. No Math.random and no clock anywhere in the engine,
// so the dealt cards, the mixed actions and the 400,000-hand witness all replay identically.
// The simulator exists only to confirm the algebra describes the game people actually play:
// |mc - exact| / se came out at 0.20 to 0.59 over four matchups.

9. What is pinned, and the one pin that is not a theorem

// Proved by exhaustion over all 4,096 pure pairs, 0 exceptions each:
//   fold the Jack facing a bet   call the King facing a bet     (both players)
// NOT dominated, and pinned anyway:
//   the first player never bets the Queen  - 2,688 of 4,096 pairs PREFER betting it
// justified instead by the 4-parameter sweep: 0 of its optima bet the Queen, at 1/12, 1/21, 1/30

The honest version of a reduced strategy space is to say which reductions are theorems and which are results. Two are theorems. The Queen-bet pin is a measurement, and the sweep that justifies it is on the page rather than in a footnote.

10. Three assumptions of mine that the engine refused

// 1. "a finer grid is a better grid"        - 1/64 returns 1/32's answer to the last bit
// 2. "two leaks cost more than one"          - 1/6 plus 1/9 measures 1/9
// 3. "the second player's floor follows the same congruence bound"
//                                            - the bound is slack by 4x; the law is measured

The first is the page. The second is a card on the PLAY tab. The third is the reason the second-player table says measured where the first-player table says proved.
