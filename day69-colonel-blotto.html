GAMEFROMZERO · DAY 69

⚔️ Colonel Blotto — the first game here where both sides move at the same time. Split your army across the battlefields, your opponent splits theirs, both sealed; whoever sends more soldiers takes the field, and whoever takes more fields wins. No turn order means no tree, no side to move and no best move: every allocation you can name has a counter that beats it outright, so the best guaranteed pure result is −1 while the value of the game is 0 — randomising is worth one whole battle. What the engine returns is therefore not a move but a distribution, and the way to get one exactly is linear programming: the two players' programs are LP duals of each other, and that duality is von Neumann's minimax theorem. The simplex runs in exact BigInt fractions and is then thrown away — what it emits is a certificate, two mixed strategies and a value, and the page re-checks the certificate with arithmetic that has never heard of a pivot. The matrix is also symmetric under permuting equally-valued fields, so the 28 allocations of six soldiers collapse to 7 shapes; the LP solves the small game and the answer is lifted back and certified against the big matrix. Then the part that is measured rather than claimed: against a bot playing that equilibrium, two of the five naive strategies score exactly 0 — the value of the game, indistinguishable from perfect play — and one of those two loses every single battle to an opponent that has merely watched its distribution. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

Delete the turn order, and the answer stops being a move


      
Every engine in this series so far has been able to ask one question: given this
      position, what should I do. Day 68 took the turn variable out of the state and the
      answer became a price. Colonel Blotto takes something else away — the order.
      Both armies are committed at the same instant. Neither side sees the other's orders. There
      is no "position after my move" to evaluate, because there is no such thing as my move
      happening first.


      
And the moment you try to name a best allocation, the game refuses. Take three fields
      and six soldiers. 4–1–1 beats 3–3–0, which
      beats 2–2–2, which beats 4–1–1 —
      a rock-paper-scissors triangle in an army's worth of soldiers, and the page counts
      180 such triangles among the 28 allocations. Every single one of those 28
      has something that beats it outright, so the best you can guarantee with a fixed
      plan is to lose:


      
max over my allocations   min over theirs   =  -1        (pick anything, they answer it)
min over their allocations max over mine    =  +1        (they pick, I answer)
                       the value of the game =   0


      
That gap of 2 is not a rounding problem or a weak evaluation function. It is the whole
      reason mixed strategies exist. The number in the middle only appears once you are allowed
      to be unpredictable, and the engine's job is to find how unpredictable, exactly.


      
The engine is a linear program, and duality is the theorem


      
Write down what the row player wants. Choose weights x over allocations,
      non-negative, summing to one, and maximise the worst column:


      
max v   s.t.   sum_i x_i A_ij  >=  v   for every enemy allocation j
               sum_i x_i = 1,   x >= 0


      
Shift the matrix so every entry is positive (adding a constant to a payoff matrix adds
      the same constant to its value), substitute w = x / v, and the normalisation turns
      into the objective:


      
min 1'w   s.t.  A'w >= 1,  w >= 0                 the row player
max 1'z   s.t.  A'z <= 1,  z >= 0                 the column player


      
Those two are an LP dual pair. Strong duality says their optima are equal — and that
      equality, spelled out in terms of the game, says that the best the row player can
      guarantee equals the best the column player can hold them to. That is the minimax theorem.
      It is not proved using linear programming here; in this formulation it is
      linear programming. And because the second form has all-positive right-hand sides, the
      origin is feasible, there is no phase 1 to write, and one primal solve returns both
      strategies: the primal variables rescaled are the column mix, the dual prices rescaled are
      the row mix.


      
Why the arithmetic has to be exact


      
The values that come out are fractions like 4/9, 13/21, 53/115. Worse, an equilibrium is
      a system of equalities — the opponent is made indifferent across their support —
      and which strategies are in that support is decided by sign tests on the solution. One
      rounded comparison and a strategy silently enters or leaves the support; the value drifts by
      10−16 and the structure of the answer changes. So every number the
      solver compares is a ratio of two BigInts in lowest terms, and the ratio test compares by
      cross-multiplication rather than dividing.


      
Exactness also buys the anti-cycling rule. A Blotto tableau is enormously degenerate —
      whole blocks of identical ratios, because the payoffs are only −1, 0 and +1 — which
      is exactly the condition under which a naive simplex can cycle forever. The leaving row is
      picked lexicographically over the slack columns, which are the rows of B−1
      and therefore linearly independent, so the tie always resolves and the objective is
      strictly lexicographically increasing. No perturbation, no epsilon, no iteration cap doing
      the work of a proof.


      
The simplex is not trusted


      

        
An equilibrium is self-certifying. If x and
        y are distributions, and the row player's worst column under x equals
        the column player's best row under y equals v, then neither side can
        deviate profitably and v is the value — by definition, no matter how the pair was
        found. So the LP's job ends at "produce a candidate". The page then verifies it with a
        loop over the full matrix that contains no pivots, no tableau and no duality.

      


      
This is the difference between a program you believe and a program you check. A search
      engine's output cannot be verified without re-searching. An LP's can: the certificate is
      smaller than the computation that produced it.


      
455 allocations, 34 shapes


      
All the battlefields are worth the same, so permuting them maps allocations to
      allocations and leaves every payoff alone. If x is optimal then so is every
      permutation of x, and since optimal strategies form a convex set, the average over
      all N! permutations is optimal too — and that average is uniform inside each
      orbit. So there is always an optimal strategy that plays a shape
      (5–3–1) and then shuffles which field gets which number.


      
Restricting both players to such strategies cannot change the value, so the LP can be
      solved on shapes instead of allocations: partitions instead of compositions. Four fields
      and twelve soldiers is 455 allocations and 34 shapes. The quotient payoff is a
      representative of shape i against the uniform mix on shape j, multiplied
      through by the group order so it stays integral — every orbit size divides |G|.


      
And the symmetry is a symmetry of the worth vector, not of the board.
      Set one field's value to 2 and the group collapses; set them to 1, 2, 3 and it collapses to
      the identity, the reduction evaporates, and the page has to solve the full matrix. You can
      watch that happen in the dropdown.


      
The measurement that decides what this page says


      
Five strategies, judged twice. Against a bot playing the exact equilibrium,
      "spread evenly" (2–2–2) scores 0.0000 — the value of the game,
      indistinguishable from perfect play. So does "two fronts". Against an opponent who has
      simply watched the distribution and best-responds to it, 2–2–2 scores
      −1: it loses every battle, forever, to 3–3–0.


      

        
An equilibrium opponent does not punish you. It only declines to lose.
        Playing a strong bot to a draw is therefore no evidence at all that your strategy
        is sound — and the page prints both numbers side by side for exactly that reason. The
        quantity that matters is exploitability, which is not a score against
        anybody: it is the minimum over all columns, and it can be computed rather than played.

      


      
What the equilibrium actually looks like


      
Six soldiers on three fields: play 4–1–1,
      3–3–0 and 3–2–1 with probability
      1/3 each, shuffling the fields. Nine soldiers: a single shape,
      5–3–1, uniformly over its six orderings — one shape, played
      randomly, is unbeatable. And five soldiers on five fields has an optimal
      pure strategy, one soldier each, because that game does have a saddle
      point. The engine tells you which case you are in; nothing about "simultaneous moves"
      forces you to randomise.


      
One thing the page had to be talked out of claiming, though: that mixture is not
      the answer. Scan every shape on its own and 4–2–0 shuffled
      across the three fields is already unbeatable by itself — exploitability exactly 0,
      the value of the game, from a single shape the LP never put any weight on. The value of a
      matrix game is unique; an optimal strategy is not, and re-solving with the shapes in a
      different order returns a different vertex of the same optimal face. So the page reports
      what it found and how many other supports it found, rather than presenting one
      vertex as the solution.


      
One more soldier is worth a lot


      
Equal armies of six on three fields: value 0. Seven against six: value
      1/3 — you win two battles for every one you lose. Below a threshold the
      value is exactly −1 (the enemy has a pure allocation that beats everything you own),
      above another it is exactly +1, and in between it is a fraction that the LP produces and
      nothing simpler predicts. The naive guess for the upper threshold is "twice the army": you
      put S+1 on two fields and win them both. Measured, on three fields, it is
      ⌈1.5 S⌉ — 9 soldiers beat 6, not 14 — and the page checks
      that number two independent ways, the LP value hitting exactly 1 and a search for a single
      allocation that beats every enemy allocation.


    

  






  

    


      
🔨 How it is built


      
1. A strategy is a composition, and there are C(S+N−1, N−1) of them

      
function compositions(S, N){        // every way to split S soldiers over N fields
  ...  for (let v = left; v >= 0; v--){ cur[pos] = v; rec(pos + 1, left - v); }
}
compositions(6, 3).length === 28 === binom(8, 2)

      
The enumeration order is fixed and the count is asserted against the binomial, because
      every index on this page — matrix rows, orbit members, policy weights — is a position in
      that list.


      
2. Rationals first, and only then a simplex

      
function fr(n, d){                   // BigInt / BigInt, lowest terms, positive denominator
  if (d < 0n){ n = -n; d = -d; }
  const g = bgcd(n, d);
  return { n: n / g, d: d / g };
}
function fcmp(a, b){ const l = a.n * b.d, r = b.n * a.d; return l < r ? -1 : (l > r ? 1 : 0); }

      
There is no float anywhere in the solver. Values like 13/21 and 53/115 are not
      representable, and the support of an equilibrium is decided by sign tests, so a rounded
      comparison does not shift an answer slightly — it returns a different strategy.


      
3. The ratio test that cannot cycle

      
// ratios by cross-multiplication: T[i][RHS]/T[i][q] vs T[k][RHS]/T[k][q]
cmp = fcmp(fmul(T[i][RHS], T[k][q]), fmul(T[k][RHS], T[i][q]));
// ties broken LEXICOGRAPHICALLY over the slack columns - which are the rows of B^-1
for (let s = n; s < n + m && cmp === 0; s++)
  cmp = fcmp(fmul(T[i][s], T[p][q]), fmul(T[p][s], T[i][q]));

      
Payoffs are only −1/0/+1, so equal ratios are the rule rather than the exception —
      the default game reports a degenerate pivot on nearly every iteration. Bland's rule would
      also terminate but crawls; the lexicographic rule keeps Dantzig's entering choice and still
      makes the objective row strictly increase in lexicographic order.


      
4. One primal solve returns both players

      
// max 1'z  s.t.  A'z <= 1, z >= 0     - origin feasible, so no phase 1
const vs = fdiv(F1, s.obj);          // 1 / optimum = the shifted value
y = s.z.map(t => fmul(t, vs));       // primal  -> column player
x = s.dual.map(t => fmul(t, vs));    // duals   -> row player
value = fsub(vs, fi(shift));

      
The dual prices are read straight off the objective row's slack columns at optimality.
      Getting that wrong is the classic silent bug here: you still get a plausible value and a
      plausible-looking strategy for one player, and the other player's strategy is nonsense —
      which is precisely what the certificate catches.


      
5. The certificate, over the full matrix

      
lo = min over ALL columns j of  sum_i x_i M[i][j]
hi = max over ALL rows i    of  sum_j y_j M[i][j]
ok = distributions && lo === value && hi === value

      
Three lines, and they are the actual definition of an equilibrium. Note what is
      not in them: the tableau, the shift, the orbit reduction, the group. The lifted
      strategy is checked against the matrix the game is actually played on. A negative control
      backs this up — perturb the strategy by one unit and the certificate must fail, which the
      self-test asserts rather than assumes.


      
6. The orbit sums that make the check cheap

      
colSum[orbit(i)][j] += M[i][j];        // built once with the matrix
(x'M)_j = sum over shapes  ( x_shape / |orbit| ) * colSum[shape][j]

      
A group-uniform strategy is constant inside each orbit, so its column values need
      #shapes × n rational operations instead of m × n. Certifying
      455 allocations against 455 allocations costs 34 × 455 fractions. That is
      why the full-matrix certificate can run on every configuration change.


      
7. The quotient game, and why it is integral

      
U[i][j] = ( |G| / |orbit j| ) * sum over b in orbit j of M[rep_i][b];

      
Every orbit size divides the group order, so multiplying through by |G| keeps the
      quotient matrix in integers and scales the value by |G| — divided back out at the end. The
      representative is arbitrary, and the page asserts that: the row sums per column-orbit are
      identical for every member of a row orbit, not just the one that got picked.


      
8. Two independent solvers, because agreement has to mean something

      
supportSolve : guess a support, solve the indifference system by exact Gauss,
               test the guess globally. No pivots, no duality, no shifting.
regretMatching : both sides play proportional to positive regret, average
                 the strategies. Floats, iterative, converges like 1/sqrt(T).

      
Support enumeration is exponential and is pointed only at tiny games — all 81
      2×2 matrices over −1/0/1, all 729 2×3s, and behind a button all 19,683
      3×3s. Regret matching is not exact and is not meant to be; it is the control that
      shows what solving buys over learning.


      
9. Policies weighed exactly, not sampled

      
// a policy is integer numerators over one common denominator
exploitability = min over j of ( sum_i num_i * M[i][j] ) / den      // an exact fraction
// and the same policy is then PLAYED, so the sampled mean has to land on it

      
"Spread evenly" is exactly −1 exploitable and exactly 0 against the equilibrium;
      both numbers are fractions, not averages. The sampled rounds are there to confirm the
      arithmetic describes the game being played, and the self-test checks the sample against the
      exact value in sigmas rather than eyeballing it.


      
10. What this engine is not

      
// Exact over the whole strategy space, so the limit IS the strategy space:
// C(S+N-1, N-1) allocations, and the tableau is #shapes squared. Three fields
// and twelve soldiers is 91 allocations and 19 shapes and solves in 10 ms; the
// SAME game on the full 91x91 matrix is 180 pivots and 0.9 s, and four fields
// with eight soldiers - 165 allocations, 15 shapes, 11 ms here - takes 749
// pivots and 3 minutes done directly. Continuous Blotto is a different problem
// with a different (Roberson) answer and is not in here at all.

      
The reduction is not an optimisation detail — it is the difference between a page that
      solves four fields and a page that does not.
